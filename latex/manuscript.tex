\large A cumulative reverse-shear criterion for full-Stokes flow}

\small Correspondence: \texttt{bpl7@uw.edu}}

\begin{abstract}
Ancient ice recovered at Allan Hills extends into the Miocene, but steep layers, age reversals and small-scale folds complicate its interpretation. Existing steady-flow theory shows that isochrones cannot overturn where horizontal speed increases upward. Full-Stokes flow over rough beds can contain local extrusion, so this pointwise condition is stronger than necessary. Here, we derive a signed order factor that measures whether particles deposited in sequence retain their horizontal order along an isochrone. Stratigraphic order depends on the cumulative competition between normal and reverse vertical shear, weighted most strongly where horizontal motion is slow. Reverse shear must erase both the ordering imposed at deposition and the accumulated normal shear before a layer becomes vertical. Full-Stokes mechanics provides no universal sign guarantee, although shallow-ice flow preserves order. We derive a full-Stokes threshold, bounds for non-stagnant localized obstacles, a first-order cancellation result for periodic roughness about plug flow and a trajectory diagnostic. Idealized full-Stokes calculations then produce recumbent folds directly from an ordered surface-deposition map, without an inherited layer perturbation. These results distinguish topographic draping from overturning at Allan Hills and other old-ice sites.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} ice stratigraphy; recumbent folds; Allan Hills; NSF COLDEX; full-Stokes flow; extrusion flow; age equation; blue ice

\section{Introduction}

Recovering old ice is useful only where its age and stratigraphic context can be established. The European Beyond EPICA--Oldest Ice project used radar stratigraphy and ice-flow modelling at Little Dome C to target a continuous record extending beyond \(1~\mathrm{Ma}\) \parencite{Lilien2021,Chung2025BeyondEPICA}. Antarctic blue-ice areas provide a complementary archive: surface mass loss and upward ice motion expose discontinuous climatic intervals that can be much older than conventional vertical cores \parencite{Bintanja1999,Spaulding2012}. At Allan Hills, shallow cores first yielded a disturbed section near \(1~\mathrm{Ma}\) \parencite{Higgins2015}, then atmospheric snapshots older than \(2~\mathrm{Ma}\) \parencite{Yan2019}, and most recently Pliocene and Miocene ice and air with reported ages approaching \(6~\mathrm{Ma}\) \parencite{Shackleton2025}. These cores are a focus of NSF COLDEX, which seeks to reconstruct atmospheric composition and Antarctic climate beyond the continuous \(800~\mathrm{ka}\) ice-core record.

These records are stratigraphically difficult to interpret. Allan Hills ice is strongly thinned and locally out of order. The ALHIC1901 age--depth relation includes multiple reversals, while the deepest ice can represent very large time intervals per unit core length \parencite{Shackleton2025}. Three-dimensional electrical conductivity measurements resolve mean layer dips of approximately \(29^{\circ}\) and \(69^{\circ}\) in two recent Allan Hills cores \parencite{Kirkpatrick2025}. Microstructural observations from ALHIC1901 indicate pervasive simple shear, strongly elongated bubbles, crystal-orientation stripes, and centimetre-scale kink and z-folds \parencite{Stoll2026}. Radar and flow modelling independently show that the duration and continuity of the archive vary sharply across the blue-ice area and with basal geometry \parencite{Kehrl2018}. A recent three-dimensional full-Stokes model predicts near-stagnant basal ice upstream of the local nunatak and faster flow into the Cul-de-sac from a distinct accumulation environment \parencite{Manos2026}. Deformation and transport history therefore control which ages are present, how they are ordered and how much time a sample averages.

A basic unresolved question is whether a fixed bed obstacle in an otherwise steady glacier can create the finite-amplitude disturbance required for a recumbent fold. Flow over topography certainly bends internal layers. Full mechanical calculations predict draping over long-wavelength topography and overriding of shorter-wavelength relief \parencite{Hindmarsh2006}. Rough-bed Stokes solutions can also contain local extrusion, meaning that horizontal speed increases downward over part of the ice column \parencite{Gudmundsson1997linear,Waddington2010}. At larger bed slopes, separated secondary circulation is possible even in creeping flow \parencite{Gudmundsson1997nonlinear}. These results appear, at first sight, to undermine the common intuition that a steady obstacle can produce only a standing layer disturbance rather than a recumbent fold.

These statements concern different parts of the layer geometry. A material tangent is an infinitesimal line segment joining neighboring particles within a layer. Flow around an obstacle can bend the layer and rotate this segment locally. The layer remains ordered, however, if particles deposited at successively larger upstream positions also occur at successively larger downstream positions everywhere along an equal-age surface. A recumbent fold begins only where that horizontal ordering vanishes and then reverses. Curvature and local tangent rotation can therefore occur without an order reversal.

The local theory of \textcite{Waddington2001}, hereafter WBA, formalized how a pre-existing wrinkle is flattened by longitudinal extension or overturned by shear. WBA also stated the key qualitative principle that layers spending their entire history in a steady-state flow field are not passively folded; a transient process must first create a disturbance relative to the steady stratigraphy. \textcite{JacobsonWaddington2004} replaced the local criterion with finite-strain integration along trajectories, and \textcite{JacobsonWaddington2005} showed that divide migration can place an inherited Raymond arch in a shear regime that turns it into a recumbent fold. For steady stratigraphy, \textcite{ParreninHindmarshRemy2006} introduced logarithmic flux coordinates for analytical age solutions, and \textcite{ParreninHindmarsh2007} generalized the construction to horizontally non-uniform velocity profiles. Their Appendix A showed that an isochrone cannot pass through vertical when horizontal velocity increases monotonically upward. Corrections for flow-induced layer thinning date at least to \textcite{Nye1963} and the early depth--age model of \textcite{DansgaardJohnsen1969}. The same characteristic framework subsequently underpinned numerical age solvers \parencite{HindmarshLeysingerVieliParrenin2009,ParreninChungMartin2025} and an explicit vertical thinning function \parencite{Parrenin2013,ParreninChungMartin2025}.

Monotonic upward speed is sufficient but not necessary for preserving order because full-Stokes glacier flow does not impose \(u_z\geq0\) pointwise. The relevant quantity is cumulative: how much reverse shear can a particle experience before the depositional ordering of the isochrone is lost? We answer this question for steady two-dimensional flow by:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,label=(\roman*)]
  \item derive a physical-coordinate representation of the signed inverse vertical-thinning factor and its interpretation as horizontal order along an isochrone;
  \item express its sign as a cumulative competition between surface-fast and reverse vertical shear;
  \item decompose the reverse-shear budget using the full-Stokes stress balance;
  \item identify regimes in which ordinary glacier mechanics guarantees order and regimes in which it does not;
  \item derive a sufficient no-overturning bound for localized obstacles and a first-order cancellation result for periodic small-amplitude roughness; and
  \item formulate a direct trajectory diagnostic and construct steady full-Stokes examples in which surface-generated isochrones overturn without an inherited perturbation.
\end{enumerate}

The analysis builds directly on established results. The monotonic-shear no-overturning condition is due to \textcite{ParreninHindmarsh2007}; the order factor derived below is the reciprocal of the vertical thinning function developed by \textcite{Parrenin2013} and implemented by \textcite{ParreninChungMartin2025}; the local wrinkle criterion and finite-strain interpretation are due to WBA and \textcite{JacobsonWaddington2004}; and rough-bed extrusion is established \parencite{Gudmundsson1997linear}. Here, we write the sign of the inverse thinning factor as an exact positive--negative \(u_z/u^3\) shear budget. This form gives a full-Stokes residual threshold, localized-obstacle bounds, a restricted periodic-cancellation result and a trajectory diagnostic. The numerical examples use the diagnostic to construct steady, boundary-generated folds rather than to amplify a prescribed material wrinkle. These results extend steady-isochrone theory without introducing a new state variable or a universal no-folding theorem.

\section{Previous descriptions of stratigraphic folding}

\subsection{Passive and active folding}

Glacier stratification is a material structure produced at the surface and subsequently deformed by flow. Structural glaciology distinguishes passive deformation, in which layers are markers that do not alter the velocity field, from active folding, in which rheological or anisotropic contrasts feed back on stress and motion \parencite{Hudleston2015,JenningsHambrey2021}. The distinction matters because a prescribed smooth velocity field can amplify or overturn an existing perturbation without selecting its wavelength, whereas active deformation can generate disturbances and select spatial scales.

Microstructural observations show where stratigraphy and rheology may be mechanically coupled. At GISP2, crystal-orientation stripes coincide with localized shear and grain-scale folding, and spatial variation in stripe density may produce larger viscosity contrasts \parencite{Alley1997}. Numerical models predict that anisotropy and convergent flow can produce folds spanning scales from cloudy bands to radar reflectors \parencite{Bons2016,Bons2025,Zhang2024}. The analysis below instead treats age as a passive scalar in a prescribed velocity field. It gives a necessary kinematic test for folding, but it does not describe the growth of an active instability.

\subsection{The WBA local wrinkle criterion}

Let \(m=\delta z/\delta x\) be the slope of an infinitesimal material-line segment \(\delta\bm{x}\) in a two-dimensional velocity field \((u,w)\). The continuum-kinematic relation \(\mathrm{D}\delta\bm{x}/\mathrm{D}t=(\nabla\uvec)\delta\bm{x}\) describes the deformation of that segment \parencite{GurtinFriedAnand2010}. Taking the ratio of its vertical and horizontal components gives
\begin{equation}
\frac{\mathrm{D}m}{\mathrm{D}t}
= w_x+(w_z-u_x)m-u_zm^2.
\label{eq:wba}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:wba}, the Riccati form of the material-line equation used by WBA, is therefore standard continuum kinematics. WBA's distinctive step was to compare the rotation of a disturbed tangent with the rotation of the local steady isochrone tangent \(m_0\). A second equal-rotation direction separates disturbances that initially flatten toward \(m_0\) from those that rotate away from it. Their shear number expresses the competition between shear-driven rotation and longitudinal extension.

Equation~\eqref{eq:wba} is local. Applied pointwise along a complete curve, it contains the exact tangent kinematics of that curve. The published WBA stability diagnostic, however, does not by itself determine whether an entire equal-age surface loses its horizontal ordering. WBA emphasized this limitation over rough topography: a disturbed tangent can open relative to the local steady isochrone on the lee side of a bump while merely recovering deformation accumulated on the stoss side \parencite{Waddington2001}.

\subsection{Finite strain and steady isochrone geometry}

\textcite{JacobsonWaddington2004} integrated finite deformation along particle paths rather than relying on a homogeneous instantaneous velocity gradient. This is the appropriate framework for deciding whether an inherited open fold becomes recumbent after a long and spatially variable strain history. Their later divide-migration calculation supplied a concrete source of the inherited perturbation \parencite{JacobsonWaddington2005}.

A separate line of analysis concerns the geometry of isochrones generated continuously in a steady flow. \textcite{ParreninHindmarshRemy2006} introduced logarithmic total-flux and normalized-streamfunction coordinates in which particle trajectories are straight characteristics when the vertical velocity shape is horizontally uniform. \textcite{Hindmarsh2006} compared streamlines and isochrones over basal topography and showed that they are close when
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dot a}{U}\frac{L}{H}\ll1,
\label{eq:hindmarshscale}
\end{equation}
where \(\dot a\) is accumulation, \(U\) a horizontal speed, \(L\) the forcing wavelength and \(H\) the ice thickness. \textcite{ParreninHindmarsh2007} generalized the characteristic analysis to horizontally non-uniform velocity profiles and wrote isochrone slope as an instantaneous normalized-streamfunction contribution plus a path-history term. In their notation, the sign of \(1-\alpha\) determines whether neighboring particles preserve their order, and \(\alpha>1\) corresponds to an isochrone that has passed through vertical. Section~\ref{sec:pathfactor} relates \(1-\alpha\) directly to the order Jacobian derived below. \textcite{ParreninHindmarsh2007} proved that \(\alpha>1\) cannot occur if horizontal velocity increases upward. \textcite{HindmarshLeysingerVieliParrenin2009} used these solutions to verify a large-scale numerical age model, and \textcite{ParreninChungMartin2025} recently implemented the same flux-coordinate framework for age, particle origin and vertical thinning.

\section{Steady age transport and the order Jacobian}

\subsection{Setting and assumptions}

Consider a steady two-dimensional glacier velocity
\begin{equation}
\uvec(x,z)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
u(x,z) \\
w(x,z)
\end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
where \(u\) and \(w\) are the horizontal and vertical velocity components, respectively, and \(x\) and \(z\) are horizontal and vertical coordinates. The domain is bounded above by the steady surface \(z=s(x)\). The kinematic result requires only the following assumptions on the region and trajectories of interest:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,label=(A\arabic*)]
  \item The velocity is continuously differentiable and incompressible,
  \begin{equation}
  u_x+w_z=0.
  \label{eq:incompressible}
  \end{equation}
  \item Horizontal motion is one-way,
  \begin{equation}
  u(x,z)>0,
  \label{eq:oneway}
  \end{equation}
  after orienting \(x\) downstream.
  \item Every trajectory considered originates in one upstream surface accumulation zone. The accumulation rate, expressed in vertical ice-equivalent units, satisfies \(\dot a(x)>0\) where the trajectory enters the ice.
  \item Stratigraphic age is a passive, non-diffusive scalar. Its steady value \(\tau(x,z)\) satisfies
  \begin{equation}
  \frac{\mathrm{D}\tau}{\mathrm{D}t}
  =\underbrace{\tau_t}_{=0}+u\tau_x+w\tau_z
  =u\tau_x+w\tau_z
  =1,
  \qquad \tau=0\quad\text{at deposition}.
  \label{eq:age}
  \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

Equation~\eqref{eq:age} distinguishes a steady age field from the age of a moving parcel. The local derivative \(\tau_t\) vanishes at fixed position, while the material age increases at one unit of time per unit time along a trajectory.

An impermeable bed is not required for the local result. Basal melting may terminate some trajectories without altering the derivation before termination. Basal freezing, in contrast, introduces a second material source and requires separate origin labels. Assumption (A2) excludes horizontal stagnation and closed circulation. Assumption (A3) requires special care in a blue-ice area because ice leaves through the local surface ablation zone. Its material origin must instead be traced to an upstream surface accumulation zone or to basal freeze-on. Appendix~\ref{app:density} gives the corresponding mass-flux construction when density varies.

In a two-dimensional incompressible Stokes solution, mass conservation permits the velocity to be represented by a scalar streamfunction \(\psi\). Curves of constant \(\psi\) are streamlines because \(\uvec\cdot\nabla\psi=0\), and the difference in \(\psi\) between two streamlines is their volume flux per unit width. The streamfunction is defined up to an arbitrary additive constant and follows from incompressibility alone; no additional rheological assumption is needed. We choose the orientation
\begin{equation}
 u=\psi_z,
 \qquad
 w=-\psi_x.
 \label{eq:streamfunction}
\end{equation}
Combining Equations~\eqref{eq:age} and \eqref{eq:streamfunction} gives the exact Jacobian identity
\begin{equation}
\begingroup
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.7}
\setlength{\arraycolsep}{10pt}
\begin{vmatrix}
\displaystyle\frac{\partial\tau}{\partial x} & \displaystyle\frac{\partial\tau}{\partial z} \\
\displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x} & \displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial z}
\end{vmatrix}
=\tau_x\psi_z-\tau_z\psi_x
=u\tau_x+w\tau_z
=1.
\endgroup
\label{eq:agepsiJacobian}
\end{equation}
Thus age and streamfunction form local area-preserving coordinates. Isochrones and streamlines intersect transversely wherever the assumptions hold. This is a classical flow-coordinate construction; it is useful here because its inverse directly measures stratigraphic order. Appendix~\ref{app:inverse} gives the inverse-coordinate identities used below. Figure~\ref{fig:geometry} shows the deposition coordinate, two neighboring trajectories and their positions on an equal-age surface.

\subsection{Surface origin and travel time}

The streamfunction restricted to the surface is
\begin{equation}
Q(x)=\psi\bigl(x,s(x)\bigr).
\end{equation}
The steady surface kinematic condition is
\begin{equation}
 u_s s_x-w\bigg|_{z=s(x)}=\dot a,
\label{eq:surfacekinematic}
\end{equation}
where \(u_s=u\bigl(x,s(x)\bigr)\). Differentiating \(Q\) with the chain rule, substituting Equation~\eqref{eq:streamfunction}, and then applying Equation~\eqref{eq:surfacekinematic} gives
\begin{equation}
Q'(x)
=\psi_x\bigg|_{z=s(x)}+\psi_z\bigg|_{z=s(x)}s_x
=-w\bigg|_{z=s(x)}+u_ss_x
=\dot a(x)>0.
\label{eq:Qprime}
\end{equation}
The increment \(\dd\psi\) is the ice flux per unit width between neighboring streamlines. At the surface, this flux is \(\dot a_0\dd x_0\), so larger accumulation places the same flux interval within a smaller deposition interval. Every streamline label \(\psi\) therefore has a unique deposition position \(x_0(\psi)\) satisfying
\begin{equation}
Q(x_0)=\psi,
\qquad
\frac{\dd x_0}{\dd\psi}=\frac{1}{\dot a_0},
\label{eq:x0psi}
\end{equation}
where \(\dot a_0\equiv\left.\dot a(x)\right|_{x=x_0}=\dot a(x_0)\) is the accumulation rate at deposition.

At fixed \(\psi\), Equation~\eqref{eq:age} gives
\begin{equation}
\left.\frac{\partial\tau}{\partial x}\right|_{\psi}=\frac{1}{u}.
\end{equation}
Consequently the age at \((x,\psi)\) is the travel time from deposition. Here, \(\xi\) is the horizontal coordinate of integration and \(z(\xi,\psi)\) is the elevation of the streamline labelled by \(\psi\):
\begin{equation}
\tau(x,\psi)=\int_{x_0(\psi)}^{x}
\frac{\dd\xi}{u\left[\xi,z(\xi,\psi)\right]}.
\label{eq:traveltime}
\end{equation}
At fixed \(x\), Equation~\eqref{eq:streamfunction} implies
\begin{equation}
 z_{\psi}=\frac{1}{u}.
\label{eq:zpsi}
\end{equation}
Stratigraphic order compares travel times on neighboring streamlines, which are neighboring values of \(\psi\). We therefore differentiate Equation~\eqref{eq:traveltime} with respect to \(\psi\) at fixed downstream position. Using Equations~\eqref{eq:x0psi} and \eqref{eq:zpsi} gives
\begin{equation}
\tau_{\psi}
=-\frac{1}{\dot a_0u_{s0}}
-\int_{x_0}^{x}\frac{u_z}{u^3}\,\dd\xi,
\label{eq:taupsi}
\end{equation}
where \(u_{s0}=u\left[x_0,s(x_0)\right]\) is the horizontal speed at deposition. The first term records the separation imposed at deposition; the integral records the subsequent differential travel time between neighboring streamlines.

\subsection{Exact order theorem}

Parameterize an isochrone of fixed age by the surface deposition coordinate \(x_0\). The relevant quantity is not its curvature but the horizontal projection of its tangent.

\begin{definition}[Isochrone-order Jacobian]
The isochrone-order Jacobian is
\begin{equation}
\Jord(x,x_0)
=\left.\frac{\partial x}{\partial x_0}\right|_{\tau}.
\label{eq:Jdefinition}
\end{equation}
It measures whether particles deposited in increasing order of \(x_0\) remain in increasing downstream order on an equal-age surface.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Cumulative order criterion]
Under assumptions (A1)--(A4), the isochrone-order Jacobian is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\Jord
=\frac{u(x,z)}{u_{s0}}
\left(
1+\dot a_0u_{s0}
\int_{x_0}^{x}\frac{u_z}{u^3}\,\dd\xi
\right).
}
\label{eq:Jexact}
\end{equation}
It is also related exactly to the vertical age gradient by
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\Jord=-\dot a_0\tau_z.}
\label{eq:Jage}
\end{equation}
Hence \(\Jord>0\) denotes ordered stratigraphy, \(\Jord=0\) a vertical tangent, and \(\Jord<0\) an overturned limb.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
At fixed age, differentiation of \(\tau(x,\psi)\) gives
\begin{equation}
\left.\frac{\partial x}{\partial\psi}\right|_{\tau}
=-\frac{\tau_{\psi}}{\tau_x|_{\psi}}
=-u\tau_{\psi}.
\end{equation}
Multiplication by \(\dd\psi/\dd x_0=\dot a_0\) and substitution of Equation~\eqref{eq:taupsi} gives Equation~\eqref{eq:Jexact}. At fixed \(x\), \(\tau_z=\tau_{\psi}\psi_z=u\tau_{\psi}\), which gives Equation~\eqref{eq:Jage}.
\end{proof}

The correction of annual-layer thickness for cumulative ice deformation goes back at least to \textcite{Nye1963} and \textcite{DansgaardJohnsen1969}. In the present notation, the vertical thinning function \(\lambda_z\) is the current vertical thickness of an infinitesimal age layer divided by its ice-equivalent thickness at deposition. For an ordered layer, a positive age increment \(\dd\tau\) corresponds to the present vertical thickness \(-\dd z=-\dd\tau/\tau_z\) and the initial thickness \(\dot a_0\dd\tau\). Hence
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\lambda_z
=-\frac{1}{\dot a_0\tau_z}
=\Jord^{-1},
\qquad \Jord>0.
}
\label{eq:thinningrelation}
\end{equation}
Thus \(\Jord\) is not a new independent state variable: in the ordered regime it is the inverse of the established vertical thinning function \parencite{Parrenin2013,ParreninChungMartin2025}. Equation~\eqref{eq:Jage} provides its signed continuation through a vertical tangent. The standard positive thinning function diverges as \(\tau_z\rightarrow0\), whereas \(\Jord\) crosses zero regularly and records orientation reversal.

At deposition, \(x=x_0\), \(u=u_{s0}\), and \(\Jord=1\). At the first vertical tangent, \(\tau_z=0\), but the material curve remains regular. Inverting Equation~\eqref{eq:agepsiJacobian} shows that
\begin{equation}
\left.\frac{\partial z}{\partial x_0}\right|_{\tau}
=\dot a_0\tau_x.
\end{equation}
When \(\Jord=0\), Equation~\eqref{eq:age} gives \(\tau_x=1/u\), and therefore
\begin{equation}
\left.\frac{\partial z}{\partial x_0}\right|_{\tau,\Jord=0}
=\frac{\dot a_0}{u}>0.
\end{equation}
The divergence of the conventional slope \(m=\dd z/\dd x\) at overturning is therefore a coordinate singularity, not a singularity of the layer itself.

\subsection{Positive and reverse shear budgets}

Define
\begin{equation}
 u_z^+=\max(u_z,0),
 \qquad
 u_z^-=\max(-u_z,0),
\end{equation}
and the dimensionless cumulative contributions
\begin{align}
\Rpos(x,x_0)
&=\dot a_0u_{s0}
\int_{x_0}^{x}\frac{u_z^+}{u^3}\,\dd\xi,
\label{eq:Pdef}\\
\Rneg(x,x_0)
&=\dot a_0u_{s0}
\int_{x_0}^{x}\frac{u_z^-}{u^3}\,\dd\xi.
\label{eq:Rdef}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:Jexact} becomes
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\Jord=\frac{u}{u_{s0}}\orderfactor,
\qquad
\orderfactor\equiv1+\Rpos-\Rneg.
}
\label{eq:JPR}
\end{equation}
The dimensionless factor \(\orderfactor\) is an order budget: deposition contributes the initial value one, normal shear adds \(\Rpos\), and reverse shear subtracts \(\Rneg\). Because \(u/u_{s0}>0\), the necessary-and-sufficient condition for preservation of horizontal order is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\Rneg<1+\Rpos.}
\label{eq:ordercondition}
\end{equation}
The first vertical tangent occurs at
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\Rneg=1+\Rpos,}
\label{eq:verticalcondition}
\end{equation}
and overturning occurs when \(\Rneg>1+\Rpos\).

The unit term in Equations~\eqref{eq:JPR}--\eqref{eq:verticalcondition} represents the initial ordering imposed at deposition. Positive \(u_z\), the usual surface-fast glacier profile, adds to the budget. Reverse shear must remove both contributions. Thus a local region with \(u_z<0\) is not itself a fold criterion.

In travel-time coordinates, \(\dd x=u\,\dd t\), so
\begin{equation}
\Rneg
=\dot a_0u_{s0}
\int_0^T\frac{u_z^-}{u^2}\,\dd t.
\label{eq:Rtime}
\end{equation}
where \(T\) is the travel time from deposition to the point of interest. Reverse shear is amplified by residence time and by slow horizontal motion. This weighting explains why local extrusion in fast throughflow may leave stratigraphic order unchanged, while modest reverse shear near stagnation can dominate the order budget.

\begin{corollary}[Monotonic-shear result]
If \(u_z\geq0\) everywhere along a trajectory, then \(\Rneg=0\), \(\Jord>0\), and the steady isochrone cannot overturn.
\end{corollary}

This corollary is the physical-coordinate statement of the no-overturning result in Appendix A of \textcite{ParreninHindmarsh2007}. It is sufficient, not necessary. Equation~\eqref{eq:ordercondition} permits finite intervals of reverse shear provided their cumulative weighted contribution remains below the order budget. Figure~\ref{fig:budget} summarizes the three contributions and the point at which the budget reaches zero.

\subsection{Relation to the Parrenin--Hindmarsh path factor}
\label{sec:pathfactor}

\textcite{ParreninHindmarsh2007} used a normalized streamfunction and logarithmic flux coordinates. For two neighboring particles, their Appendix A gives a mapping of the form
\begin{equation}
\delta\pi=(1-\alpha)\,\delta\pi_0,
\end{equation}
where \(\pi_0\) and \(\pi\) are their logarithmic flux coordinates at deposition and at the point of interest, respectively, and \(\alpha\) is their path-history factor. An isochrone exceeds vertical when \(\alpha>1\). Their present and surface-origin coordinates are monotone functions of \(x\) and \(x_0\) wherever assumptions (A2)--(A3) hold. Consequently,
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{sgn}(1-\alpha)
=\operatorname{sgn}\!\left(
\left.\frac{\partial x}{\partial x_0}\right|_{\tau}
\right)
=\operatorname{sgn}(\Jord)
=\operatorname{sgn}(-\tau_z).
\label{eq:PHsign}
\end{equation}
The present result is therefore not an independent no-overturning theorem. Nor is \(\Jord\) independent of the established thinning variable: Equation~\eqref{eq:thinningrelation} identifies it as the signed inverse vertical-thinning factor. The contribution of the physical-space representation is that it exposes the positive and negative parts of vertical shear, remains regular at the first vertical tangent and can be evaluated directly from a numerical velocity field. The \texttt{age\_flow\_line} numerical age model of \textcite{ParreninChungMartin2025} assumes strictly positive accumulation for its horizontal flux coordinate and no reverse horizontal flow for its vertical flux coordinate; it computes age and positive vertical thinning within that admissible regime.

\section{Full-Stokes constraints on isochrone order}

\subsection{Exact stress decomposition of vertical shear}

The order theorem is kinematic. To determine whether glacier mechanics forces Equation~\eqref{eq:ordercondition} to hold, consider steady, isothermal or thermomechanically prescribed, isotropic generalized-Newtonian Stokes flow,
\begin{align}
\nabla\cdot\uvec&=0,
\label{eq:mass}\\
-\nabla p+\nabla\cdot\taut+\rho\bm{g}&=\bm{0},
\label{eq:momentum}\\
\taut&=2\eta\Dten,
\qquad
\Dten=\tfrac12\left(\nabla\uvec+\nabla\uvec^{\mathsf T}\right),
\qquad \eta>0.
\label{eq:rheology}
\end{align}
Here, \(p\) is pressure, \(\rho\) is ice density, \(\bm{g}\) is gravitational acceleration, \(\taut\) is deviatoric stress, \(\Dten\) is the strain-rate tensor and \(\eta>0\) is the scalar effective viscosity. The viscosity may depend on temperature and effective strain rate. Anisotropic rheology is discussed separately below.

With \(z\) vertical and gravity having no horizontal component, the horizontal momentum equation is
\begin{equation}
-p_x+\tau_{xx,x}+\tau_{xz,z}=0.
\label{eq:xmomentum}
\end{equation}
Write
\begin{equation}
p=p_{\mathrm{atm}}+\rho g(s-z)+p_{\mathrm{nh}},
\end{equation}
where \(g=|\bm{g}|\), \(p_{\mathrm{atm}}\) is atmospheric pressure and \(p_{\mathrm{nh}}\) contains all nonhydrostatic pressure, including the finite-slope surface correction. Integrating Equation~\eqref{eq:xmomentum} downward from the free surface, with \(\zeta\) as the vertical coordinate of integration, gives
\begin{equation}
\tau_{xz}(x,z)
=\tau_{xz}^{s}
-\rho g s_x(s-z)
+\int_z^s\left(\tau_{xx,x}-p_{\mathrm{nh},x}\right)\,\dd\zeta.
\label{eq:tauxzint}
\end{equation}
Because \(\tau_{xz}=\eta(u_z+w_x)\),
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
 u_z
 =\underbrace{\frac{\rho g(s-z)(-s_x)}{\eta}}_{G}
 +\underbrace{
 \left[
 -w_x+\frac{\tau_{xz}^{s}}{\eta}
 +\frac{1}{\eta}\int_z^s
 \left(\tau_{xx,x}-p_{\mathrm{nh},x}\right)\,\dd\zeta
 \right]}_{R_{\mathrm{FS}}}.
}
\label{eq:uzdecomp}
\end{equation}
where the superscript \(s\) denotes surface stress. For a surface descending downstream, \(s_x\leq0\), the gravity-driven term \(G\) is nonnegative and favors order preservation. The residual \(R_{\mathrm{FS}}\) contains the full-Stokes effects omitted from the shallow-ice shear balance: gradients of vertical velocity, longitudinal stress, nonhydrostatic pressure and the Cartesian surface shear-stress component.

Substitution into Equation~\eqref{eq:Jexact} gives
\begin{equation}
\Jord=\frac{u}{u_{s0}}
\left(1+\Ggrav+\Gfs\right),
\label{eq:Jfullstokes}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{align}
\Ggrav&=\dot a_0u_{s0}
\int_{x_0}^{x}\frac{G}{u^3}\,\dd\xi\geq0,
\label{eq:Ggrav}\\
\Gfs&=\dot a_0u_{s0}
\int_{x_0}^{x}\frac{R_{\mathrm{FS}}}{u^3}\,\dd\xi.
\label{eq:Gfs}
\end{align}
The exact full-Stokes condition for a vertical or overturned isochrone is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\Gfs\leq-\left(1+\Ggrav\right).
}
\label{eq:FSfailure}
\end{equation}
A conservative sufficient condition for no overturning is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\dot a_0u_{s0}
\int_{x_0}^{x}
\frac{\negpart{R_{\mathrm{FS}}}}{u^3}\,\dd\xi<1.
}
\label{eq:FSsufficient}
\end{equation}
This inequality discards all positive contributions, including gravity-driven shear, and is therefore stronger than necessary but easy to test.

\subsection{There is no universal full-Stokes sign theorem}

Neither incompressibility nor the Stokes equations fixes the sign of \(u_z\). The constitutive relation constrains \(u_z+w_x\), while longitudinal stress and nonhydrostatic pressure can alter the Cartesian vertical gradient of horizontal speed. Analytical rough-bed solutions predict local velocity maxima and minima, and therefore regions with \(u_z<0\), even for gravity-driven linear viscous flow \parencite{Gudmundsson1997linear}. The historical extrusion-flow literature likewise shows that surface-fast velocity profiles are not a pointwise theorem of full-Stokes mechanics \parencite{Waddington2010}.

At stronger roughness, the one-way-flow assumption itself can fail. \textcite{Gudmundsson1997nonlinear} found separated secondary circulation in troughs of sufficiently steep sinusoidal beds. Ideal no-slip Stokes flow near a sharp corner can contain nested Moffatt eddies \parencite{Moffatt1964}; \textcite{MeyerCreyts2017} extended this mechanism to Glen-law ice in subglacial mountain valleys and showed that oblique flow can produce three-dimensional eddies. These examples are not direct models of Allan Hills: glacier beds may slide, Glen flow is nonlinear, and real corners are rounded. Their theoretical significance is that steady creeping-flow equations do not forbid recirculation. When \(u=0\) or trajectories close, the downstream travel-time coordinate and unique surface-origin map cease to exist, so Equations~\eqref{eq:Jexact}--\eqref{eq:ordercondition} no longer apply globally.

The distinction between local extrusion and cumulative order reversal is central. Published rough-bed solutions establish that \(u_z\) can be negative. They do not, by that fact alone, establish that \(\Rneg\) reaches \(1+\Rpos\). The latter is an order-one integrated threshold.

\section{Regimes that preserve order}

\subsection{Shallow-ice flow}

Under the conventional shallow-ice approximation,
\begin{equation}
\tau_{xz}\simeq-\rho g(s-z)s_x.
\label{eq:SIAstress}
\end{equation}
For downstream flow with \(s_x<0\), \(\tau_{xz}>0\). An isotropic Glen-type rheology gives \(u_z\) with the same sign as \(\tau_{xz}\), while leading-order basal sliding contributes a depth-independent horizontal speed \parencite{CuffeyPaterson2010}. Therefore
\begin{equation}
 u_z\geq0,
 \qquad \Rneg=0,
 \qquad \Jord>0.
\label{eq:SIAorder}
\end{equation}
A fixed bed obstacle can produce arches, troughs and finite curvature in shallow-ice stratigraphy, but it cannot overturn a steady isochrone under these assumptions.

\subsection{Regular thin-film full-Stokes flow}

Let
\begin{equation}
\varepsilon=\frac{H}{L}\ll1
\end{equation}
be the aspect ratio of a smooth glacier. Higher-order glacier models reproduce the Stokes solution to second order in \(\varepsilon\) under their regular asymptotic assumptions, including a range of basal slip regimes \parencite{Blatter1995,SchoofHindmarsh2010}. Suppose the non-shallow contribution scales as
\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{FS}}=O\left(\varepsilon^2\frac{U}{H}\right)
\label{eq:RFSscale}
\end{equation}
through a region of length \(L=H/\varepsilon\), and suppose \(u\) remains comparable to \(U\). Then
\begin{equation}
\Gfs=O\left(\frac{\dot a}{U}\varepsilon\right).
\label{eq:GFSscale}
\end{equation}
Thus small regular full-Stokes corrections cannot erase the initial depositional contribution when
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dot a}{U}\varepsilon\ll1.
\label{eq:thinorder}
\end{equation}
This is an asymptotic sufficient regime, not a uniform theorem. The approximation can break down near divides, abrupt sliding transitions, contact zones, steep obstacles, very slow flow or other regions where the thin-film expansion is singular or nonuniform.

\subsection{A sufficient bound for a localized obstacle}

The cumulative criterion yields a direct obstacle bound without requiring pointwise monotonic shear.

\begin{proposition}[Localized reverse-shear bound]
Suppose reverse shear is confined to a trajectory segment of horizontal length \(\ell\). On that segment, assume
\begin{equation}
 u\geq U_{\min}>0,
 \qquad
 u_{s0}\leq U_{\mathrm{dep}},
 \qquad
 u_z^-\leq \dot\gamma_{\mathrm{rev}},
\label{eq:boundassumptions}
\end{equation}
where \(U_{\min}\) is the minimum horizontal speed on the segment, \(U_{\mathrm{dep}}\) bounds the horizontal speed at deposition, and \(\dot\gamma_{\mathrm{rev}}\) bounds the reverse vertical shear rate. Then
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\Rneg\leq
\frac{\dot a_0U_{\mathrm{dep}}\dot\gamma_{\mathrm{rev}}\ell}
{U_{\min}^{3}}.
}
\label{eq:obstaclebound}
\end{equation}
A sufficient condition for no overturning is that the right-hand side be less than one.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Substitute Equation~\eqref{eq:boundassumptions} into Equation~\eqref{eq:Rdef} and bound the integral by its maximum integrand multiplied by \(\ell\).
\end{proof}

For an isolated obstacle whose horizontal and vertical influence scales are both \(O(H)\), with non-stagnant throughflow,
\begin{equation}
\Rneg=O\left(\frac{\dot a}{U}\right).
\label{eq:isolatedscale}
\end{equation}
In a typical interior or flank setting where \(U\gg\dot a\), an isolated fixed bump can create local extrusion and visible draping while remaining far from the reverse-shear value required for a vertical tangent. More generally, setting the right-hand side of Equation~\eqref{eq:obstaclebound} equal to one and solving for length gives
\begin{equation}
\ell
\gtrsim
\frac{U_{\min}^{3}}
{\dot a_0U_{\mathrm{dep}}\dot\gamma_{\mathrm{rev}}}.
\label{eq:lengththreshold}
\end{equation}
Slow throughflow reduces this required length through the \(U_{\min}^{3}\) numerator. For scale, take \(\dot a_0=0.05~\mathrm{m\,yr^{-1}}\), \(U_{\mathrm{dep}}=10~\mathrm{m\,yr^{-1}}\), \(\dot\gamma_{\mathrm{rev}}=0.01~\mathrm{yr^{-1}}\) and \(\ell=1~\mathrm{km}\). Equation~\eqref{eq:obstaclebound} gives \(\Rneg\leq0.005\) when \(U_{\min}=10~\mathrm{m\,yr^{-1}}\), which rules out overturning under these assumed bounds. With \(U_{\min}=1~\mathrm{m\,yr^{-1}}\), the bound is \(\Rneg\leq5\) and no longer rules it out. These illustrative values are not estimates for Allan Hills; they show that a tenfold reduction in minimum speed raises the bound by a factor of one thousand.

Equation~\eqref{eq:obstaclebound} makes precise why ``flow over a bump cannot fold an isochrone'' is often a good glacier approximation but not a universal Stokes theorem. The approximation depends on non-stagnant flow, a localized deformation zone and a small accumulation-to-speed ratio.

\subsection{First-order cancellation over periodic roughness about plug flow}

A second reason that fixed roughness may have weak cumulative effect is cancellation, but the result requires a plug-flow background. Let
\begin{equation}
 u(x,z;\epsilon_b)=U_b+\epsilon_b u_1(x,z)+O(\epsilon_b^2),
 \qquad U_b>0,
\label{eq:plugperturbation}
\end{equation}
where \(\epsilon_b\) is a small amplitude parameter, \(U_b>0\) is spatially uniform, \(u_1\) is periodic in \(x\), and \(k\) is its wavenumber. The perturbation has wavelength \(2\pi/k\) and zero horizontal mean at each elevation. Along the unperturbed trajectory \(z=z_0\),
\begin{equation}
\frac{u_z}{u^3}
=\epsilon_b\frac{u_{1,z}(x,z_0)}{U_b^3}+O(\epsilon_b^2).
\label{eq:periodicshear}
\end{equation}
Therefore, over one complete wavelength,
\begin{equation}
\int_{x}^{x+2\pi/k}\frac{u_z}{u^3}\,\dd\xi=O(\epsilon_b^2).
\label{eq:periodiccancel}
\end{equation}
Appendix~\ref{app:periodic} gives the expansion leading to Equation~\eqref{eq:periodiccancel}. The perturbative frictionless rough-bed solution of \textcite{Gudmundsson1997linear} has this plug-flow structure: local extrusion occurs, but its first-order stoss- and lee-side contributions cancel over a complete symmetric wavelength.

Cancellation is not guaranteed for a sheared background, a solitary asymmetric obstacle, a finite path ending mid-cycle, strongly nonlinear roughness or near-stagnant trajectories. Equation~\eqref{eq:periodiccancel} is therefore a restricted asymptotic result, not a general symmetry theorem.

\section{Overturning mechanisms and limits of the assumptions}

The theorem does not fail when assumptions (A1)--(A4) hold. Instead, it predicts whether the order budget remains positive or reaches the vertical-tangent threshold. This section first describes overturning within the theorem and then identifies settings in which one or more assumptions are not satisfied, so that the two-dimensional steady coordinate construction does not apply.

\subsection{Overturning within the theorem: cumulative extrusion}

The direct steady-flow mechanism is cumulative reverse shear. A glacier with \(u>0\) can overturn a steady isochrone if reverse shear is sufficiently strong, sufficiently persistent or sufficiently concentrated where \(u\) is small. Equation~\eqref{eq:verticalcondition} gives the exact threshold:
\begin{equation}
\dot a_0u_{s0}
\int_{x_0}^{x}\frac{u_z^-}{u^3}\,\dd\xi
=
1+
\dot a_0u_{s0}
\int_{x_0}^{x}\frac{u_z^+}{u^3}\,\dd\xi.
\label{eq:exactthresholdexpanded}
\end{equation}
A change in boundary conditions is not mechanically necessary if a steady full-Stokes velocity field satisfies this equality. This outcome is predicted by the theorem rather than representing a failure of it. The extrusion-flow literature establishes the necessary local reverse shear but does not evaluate this cumulative isochrone-order threshold.

\subsection{Stagnation, separation and closed circulation}

If \(u\) reaches zero, the \(x\)-travel-time representation becomes singular. If a secondary circulation forms, a streamline may not have a unique surface origin and may intersect an equal-age surface in a topology not represented by a single downstream coordinate. Strong roughness, cavities and sharp corners are therefore not merely large values of \(\Rneg\); they can invalidate the coordinate system on which the theorem rests. Numerical calculations should treat \(u=0\) as an event and switch to a more general Lagrangian deformation analysis.

\subsection{Surface ablation and sublimation}

Newly deposited material enters the glacier through a surface accumulation zone with \(\tau=0\). Existing isochrones terminate where ice emerges in a surface ablation zone. In a blue-ice area, the surface streamfunction need not be globally monotone because
\begin{equation}
Q'(x)=\dot a(x)
\end{equation}
changes sign between accumulation and ablation zones. A streamline may enter through an upstream accumulation zone and emerge through a downstream ablation zone. The theorem can still be applied along an interior trajectory if that trajectory has a unique origin in the upstream accumulation zone, but \(Q^{-1}\) must be constructed only over that part of the surface. Multiple surface accumulation zones or basal freeze-on require a more general material-source label.

Steady ablation most naturally produces upward-bent and truncated layers, not an intact fold. However, migration, onset or cessation of an ablation zone changes the age field relative to the new steady solution and supplies the inherited disturbance considered by WBA. Surface sublimation followed by burial can also preserve an unconformity with steep or truncated limbs. At Allan Hills, present ablation terminates trajectories, whereas changes in the position of the ablation zone introduce time dependence that lies outside the steady theorem.

\subsection{Basal melting and freeze-on}

Basal melting removes trajectories and truncates layers. Basal freeze-on adds ice at the bed, so one surface deposition coordinate cannot label the entire stratigraphy. Numerical models show that freeze-on can generate plume-like structures and complex internal layering extending through a large fraction of the ice thickness \parencite{LeysingerVieli2018}. A moving melt/freeze boundary additionally makes the velocity and age problem time dependent.

\subsection{Time-dependent geometry and basal traction}

If the bed, free surface, divide position or basal traction changes, the inherited age field is not the steady age solution of the new velocity field. The perturbation then obeys a homogeneous advection equation only after the change; it need not vanish initially. Divide migration converts a formerly steady Raymond arch into a fold seed \parencite{JacobsonWaddington2005}. Traveling slippery or sticky patches produce moving-frame critical layers and thickness-scale overturning \parencite{Wolovick2014,WolovickCreyts2016}. Bedrock uplift, rotation or erosion similarly changes the reference stratigraphy and can inject a finite-amplitude disturbance even when subsequent flow is steady.

\subsection{Active rheology, anisotropy and three-dimensional flow}

Equation~\eqref{eq:uzdecomp} assumes isotropic scalar viscosity. Crystal fabric, impurities, grain size and damage can alter both the stress--strain relation and the velocity field in response to stratigraphy. Full-Stokes anisotropic divide models already demonstrate substantial changes in isochrone shape and age--depth relations \parencite{MartinGudmundssonPritchardGagliardini2009}. More strongly coupled heterogeneity can generate folds internally rather than merely advecting an imposed perturbation \parencite{Alley1997,ThorsteinssonWaddington2002,Bons2016,Bons2025}. The Allan Hills microstructure indicates that anisotropy and localized shear cannot be ignored \parencite{Stoll2026}.

The order theorem is also intrinsically two-dimensional. In three dimensions, incompressibility provides a vector potential rather than a scalar streamfunction, and neighboring surface origins can exchange order in one horizontal projection while remaining separated in the other. Divide models show that along-ridge flow and divide migration alter isochrone architecture even when cross-flow is modest \parencite{MartinHindmarshNavarro2009}. A three-dimensional generalization should track the two-dimensional Jacobian mapping a surface deposition patch to an equal-age surface. The scalar \(\Jord\) is recovered only when cross-flow is negligible and a coherent flow tube exists.

\section{Implications for old-ice sites}

Allan Hills lies close to several limits in which a two-dimensional fixed-obstacle approximation becomes unreliable. Present-day ice rises into a surface ablation zone above steep nunatak topography \parencite{Spaulding2012,Kehrl2018,Stoll2026}. The three-dimensional full-Stokes model of \textcite{Manos2026} predicts nearly stagnant basal ice upstream of the nunatak, where the bottom \(20~\mathrm{m}\) averages approximately \(0.4\)--\(0.5~\mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}\). Modeled speed remains nonzero but locally falls to order micrometres per year. In contrast, modeled basal speeds in the Cul-de-sac are approximately \(46\)--\(49~\mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}\). These modeled scales distinguish local preservation in slow ice from transport of old ice into the Cul-de-sac.

Near-stagnant flow has two consequences for the present calculation. First, the \(u^{-3}\) weighting in Equation~\eqref{eq:Rdef} becomes large and numerically sensitive even though assumption (A2) may remain technically satisfied. Second, the modeled flow is fundamentally three-dimensional. It contains important local bridging stresses associated with horizontal gradients of vertical velocity; the corresponding \(-w_x\) term appears explicitly in the full-Stokes shear decomposition, Equation~\eqref{eq:uzdecomp}. A two-dimensional order budget is therefore meaningful only within a coherent flow tube for which cross-flow is small. The Cul-de-sac presents a separate provenance problem: its geochemistry and modeled transport indicate a distinct accumulation environment \parencite{Manos2026}. The assumption that each trajectory can be represented by one surface accumulation zone is therefore unlikely to describe all Cul-de-sac ice, and a three-dimensional, multi-source material map is needed there.

The structural observations constrain, but do not determine, the deformation history. The \(69^{\circ}\) mean layer dip in ALHIC2302 is steep but remains below vertical \parencite{Kirkpatrick2025}. Centimetre-scale z-folds in ALHIC1901 record local folding, but they do not by themselves explain every core-scale age reversal \parencite{Stoll2026}. The inverted age--depth relation in ALHIC1901 more directly indicates that \(\tau_z\) changes sign over some sampled intervals \parencite{Shackleton2025}. Equation~\eqref{eq:Jage} then implies \(\Jord<0\) if the samples lie on one continuous family of surface-deposited layers. Multiple source regions, unconformities or faults could instead produce an apparent age reversal.

The same calculation is relevant to deep old-ice drilling. At the Beyond EPICA site, radar and flow modelling indicate strong basal thinning and possible stagnant or disturbed ice near the bed \parencite{Lilien2021,Chung2025BeyondEPICA}. Evaluating \(\Jord\) along model trajectories would test whether the steady velocity field preserves order above this basal unit. The boundary conditions differ from those at Allan Hills, but both settings require a mechanical link between modelled flow and the stratigraphic age interpretation.

For old-ice stratigraphic interpretation, the equations define four tests of whether steady local topography can produce an observed fold or age reversal.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,label=(\arabic*)]
  \item \textbf{Steady fixed geometry.} Compute the present or reconstructed steady full-Stokes field and evaluate \(\Rpos\), \(\Rneg\) and \(\Jord\) along source-to-core trajectories. If the order budget \(1+\Rpos-\Rneg\) remains well above zero, fixed present-day geometry cannot produce the observed recumbent folding.
  \item \textbf{Steady but singular or multi-source flow.} Test for stagnation, recirculation, basal freeze-on and surface origin multiplicity. If these occur, the simple order theorem is inapplicable and the full material map must be used.
  \item \textbf{Inherited unsteady stratigraphy.} If the steady model preserves order, evaluate changes in ablation pattern, ice thickness, flow direction, divide position, bed geometry and basal traction. These mechanisms inject finite disturbances that can later be amplified by shear.
  \item \textbf{Active deformation.} If kinematic models underpredict observed folds, couple fabric and rheological heterogeneity to flow. The microstructural observations make this mechanism plausible at centimetre scale.
\end{enumerate}

This hierarchy distinguishes regimes in which changing boundary conditions are mechanically required. In regular, shallow, non-stagnant flow, Equation~\eqref{eq:obstaclebound} keeps the order budget well above zero, so a time-dependent or rheological mechanism is needed. In more general full-Stokes flow, sufficiently cumulative extrusion or loss of one-way flow can overturn steady isochrones without a boundary change.

\section{Numerical diagnostic for full-Stokes models}

The order criterion can be evaluated without explicitly solving the age equation or advecting a dense family of isochrones. Start trajectories in a surface accumulation zone and record \(x_0\), \(\dot a_0\) and \(u_{s0}\). With \(t\) denoting time since deposition, integrate along each trajectory
\begin{align}
\frac{\dd x}{\dd t}&=u,
&
\frac{\dd z}{\dd t}&=w,
\label{eq:trajectory}\\
\frac{\dd\Rpos}{\dd t}
&=\dot a_0u_{s0}\frac{u_z^+}{u^2},
&
\frac{\dd\Rneg}{\dd t}
&=\dot a_0u_{s0}\frac{u_z^-}{u^2},
\label{eq:PRode}
\end{align}
with \(\Rpos(0)=\Rneg(0)=0\). Then
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\Jord(t)=\frac{u(t)}{u_{s0}}
\left[1+\Rpos(t)-\Rneg(t)\right].
}
\label{eq:Jdiagnostic}
\end{equation}
The diagnostic is
\begin{equation}
\min_t\left[1+\Rpos(t)-\Rneg(t)\right]
\begin{cases}
>0, & \text{order preserved},\\
=0, & \text{first vertical tangent},\\
<0, & \text{overturned steady isochrone}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:numericaltest}
\end{equation}

A robust implementation should include four checks. First, compare Equation~\eqref{eq:Jdiagnostic} with \(-\dot a_0\tau_z\) from an independently solved steady age equation. Second, advect at least one pair of neighboring particles to verify the sign of \(\partial x/\partial x_0|_{\tau}\). Third, stop the downstream-coordinate integration when \(u\) approaches zero and diagnose recirculation with the full trajectory topology. Fourth, compute \(u_z\) using the finite-element gradient rather than differencing noisy interpolated velocities. Separate storage of \(\Rpos\) and \(\Rneg\) is preferable to integrating their difference because large compensating contributions can otherwise obscure numerical error.

For transient flow, Equation~\eqref{eq:Jdiagnostic} is not valid because the steady travel-time representation fails. The corresponding calculation should integrate the deformation of the two-parameter deposition map through time. Nevertheless, the steady order budget remains useful as a baseline and as a diagnostic applied to quasi-steady snapshots.

\section{Numerical Examples}
\label{sec:numericalexamples}

We use two-dimensional full-Stokes calculations to construct recumbent folds without prescribing an internal layer perturbation. These calculations isolate mechanisms in controlled geometries; they are not reconstructions of Allan Hills or another old-ice site. The domain is periodic, \(800~\mathrm{m}\) long and \(160~\mathrm{m}\) thick, with a flat traction-free surface and a smooth raised-cosine bed obstacle. Ice is incompressible and linear viscous, with \(\eta=2.5\times10^8~\mathrm{Pa\,yr}\) and \(\rho=917~\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\). Gravity acts at a mean surface slope of \(0.03~\mathrm{rad}\). We solve for velocity and pressure with Taylor--Hood elements, using quadratic velocity and linear pressure.

The basal boundary is a sliding end member. The bed is fixed, the horizontal basal ice speed is \(U_b=0.20~\mathrm{m\,yr^{-1}}\), and no penetration gives
\begin{equation}
\uvec_b
=U_b
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
b_x
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:numericalsliding}
\end{equation}
This condition removes the large surface-fast shear generated by a no-slip bed and allows form drag to control the deformation around the obstacle. It is a specified basal-velocity model, not a sliding-law prediction. The flat surface is held steady by the diagnosed accumulation rate \(\dot a=-w\big|_{z=H}\). We use only surface intervals where \(\dot a>0\) and trajectories on which \(u>0\).

The numerical isochrones are generated from the deposition boundary. At age zero, a particle labelled by \(x_0\) is placed at \([x_0,H]^{\mathsf T}\). Integrating the steady velocity for a common age \(\tau\) gives the material curve \([x(x_0,\tau),z(x_0,\tau)]^{\mathsf T}\). We do not initialize an internal horizontal layer, wrinkle or fold. Therefore, a negative value of \(\partial x/\partial x_0|_\tau\) measures order reversal created between the surface and the plotted isochrone.

The first example isolates persistent extrusion. The obstacle is \(150~\mathrm{m}\) high, with horizontal half-widths of \(300~\mathrm{m}\) and \(80~\mathrm{m}\). Surface-generated isochrones remain ordered at \(1~\mathrm{kyr}\), first contain an overturned interval by \(2~\mathrm{kyr}\), and have a clear recumbent limb at \(2.5~\mathrm{kyr}\) (Figures~\ref{fig:numericalfolds}a--b). The minimum material-map Jacobian at \(2.5~\mathrm{kyr}\) is \(-0.455\) on the finest mesh. Along the corresponding trajectory, horizontal speed does not fall below its value at deposition. Reverse shear nevertheless removes the initial order contribution because it persists along the path.

The second example isolates slow-flow amplification. The \(140~\mathrm{m}\)-high obstacle has half-widths of \(45~\mathrm{m}\) and \(260~\mathrm{m}\). A surface-origin trajectory slows substantially upstream of the steep face while retaining \(u>0\). The corresponding isochrone reaches vertical by approximately \(0.7~\mathrm{kyr}\) and has a minimum material-map Jacobian of \(-0.270\) at \(0.8~\mathrm{kyr}\) (Figures~\ref{fig:numericalfolds}c--d). This calculation realizes the residence-time mechanism in Equation~\eqref{eq:Rtime}: slow horizontal motion gives reverse shear greater weight even though the velocity field remains steady.

These examples are constructive rather than site specific. Their topographic relief is large, the basal speed is prescribed and the rheology is linear viscous. They show that the steady age field itself can fold when cumulative reverse shear crosses the threshold; they do not show that this mechanism produced the observed Allan Hills stratigraphy. Applying the calculation at Allan Hills requires measured three-dimensional geometry, a basal traction model and source-to-core trajectories consistent with the observed surface mass balance.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{A theorem about deposition order, not about curvature}

The curvature equation for an advected material line contains first and second spatial derivatives of velocity. It correctly shows that an obstacle can create curvature from an initially straight layer. The present order criterion answers a different question. A recumbent fold requires the horizontal projection of an equal-age surface to reverse, or equivalently \(\tau_z\) to change sign. An obstacle may generate large curvature while \(\Jord\) remains positive everywhere. This is the rigorous sense in which a steady bump can disturb isochrones without supplying the order-reversing seed required for a recumbent fold.

WBA's statement that steady-state layers are not passively folded can now be made conditional. If a unique steady age field exists and Equation~\eqref{eq:ordercondition} holds, the steady surface-deposition map remains ordered. If cumulative reverse shear crosses the threshold, the steady age field itself contains a fold; no perturbation relative to that solution is required. This boundary-generated problem is distinct from placing an arbitrary material line in the velocity field and asking whether an inherited wrinkle grows.

\subsection{Why local extrusion is usually insufficient}

Full-Stokes solutions contain local extrusion, but local extrusion does not imply order reversal. Equation~\eqref{eq:JPR} can be read as an order budget. Deposition supplies an initial contribution of one, surface-fast shear adds \(\Rpos\), and reverse shear subtracts \(\Rneg\). Reverse shear around a localized or periodic obstacle may also be short-lived or cancel between stoss and lee sides. Equation~\eqref{eq:obstaclebound} bounds this subtraction, while Equation~\eqref{eq:periodiccancel} gives the perturbative cancellation.

Near stagnation, the reverse-shear subtraction becomes much more sensitive to speed. The \(u^{-3}\) spatial weighting and \(u^{-2}\) temporal weighting imply that a slowly moving basal zone can accumulate a large order change even when the pointwise shear is moderate. This is also the regime in which age becomes large, numerical conditioning deteriorates and recirculation becomes possible. A steady-obstacle explanation for Allan Hills folding must therefore identify a slow reverse-shear trajectory and show that the order budget reaches zero in Equation~\eqref{eq:verticalcondition}; showing \(u_z<0\) is insufficient.

\subsection{Boundary changes as fold seeds}

Time-dependent boundary processes provide several mechanisms for creating finite inherited disturbances. Surface sublimation can truncate and steepen layers; migration of an ablation zone can leave that geometry to be buried or sheared. Basal melt and freeze-on alter material sources and sinks. Divide migration changes the reference velocity profile. Traveling traction anomalies create a moving-frame critical layer and material trapping. Bedrock uplift or rotation changes both geometry and stress. Each mechanism breaks at least one assumption behind the steady order theorem and can therefore create a nonzero perturbation relative to the steady stratigraphy.

The order framework does not identify which mechanism operated at Allan Hills. It supplies a rejection test. If a high-resolution, history-consistent full-Stokes model of fixed geometry keeps the order budget well above zero on all relevant trajectories, then steady topographic draping is excluded as the cause of recumbent age reversals. The remaining explanation must invoke time dependence, multiple material sources, active rheology, three-dimensional exchange or structural discontinuity.

\subsection{Novelty and relationship to established theory}

Several components of this manuscript are established. Equation~\eqref{eq:agepsiJacobian} is a standard consequence of steady incompressible transport. Logarithmic flux coordinates and characteristic age solutions were developed by \textcite{ParreninHindmarshRemy2006} and \textcite{ParreninHindmarsh2007}, and the latter paper contains both the monotonic-shear corollary and the path dependence of isochrone slope. The vertical thinning function was derived in the same framework by \textcite{Parrenin2013} and is now computed operationally by \textcite{ParreninChungMartin2025}; Equation~\eqref{eq:thinningrelation} shows that the present order factor is its inverse in the ordered regime. The need for a transient wrinkle and the competition between shear and extension are due to \textcite{Waddington2001}. Finite-strain amplification is treated by \textcite{JacobsonWaddington2004}. Local extrusion and rough-bed recirculation are known full-Stokes phenomena \parencite{Gudmundsson1997linear,Gudmundsson1997nonlinear}.

The analysis therefore reformulates established age--streamfunction and thinning theory for use with numerical velocity fields. Its distinct result is the physical-space identity that writes signed inverse thinning as a cumulative \(u_z/u^3\) integral. The positive--negative decomposition in Equations~\eqref{eq:Pdef}--\eqref{eq:ordercondition} then gives the full-Stokes residual threshold in Equation~\eqref{eq:FSfailure}, the localized-obstacle bound in Equation~\eqref{eq:obstaclebound}, the plug-flow cancellation result in Equation~\eqref{eq:periodiccancel} and the split trajectory diagnostic in Equation~\eqref{eq:Jdiagnostic}. These results are a cautious extension of Parrenin--Hindmarsh theory rather than a universal theorem about incompressible Stokes flow.

The idealized calculations in Section~\ref{sec:numericalexamples} provide constructive examples of steady order reversal, but they do not test Allan Hills geometry. The next step is to apply the same material-map and order-budget calculations to reconstructed geometry and flow history. An order budget that remains well above zero would exclude fixed roughness as the cause of overturning, whereas a trajectory crossing \(\Jord=0\) would identify where steady deformation is sufficient. Layer attitudes, age reversals and fabric provide independent observations against which to evaluate either result.

\subsection{Limitations}

The analysis neglects diffusion of age markers, fracture and discontinuous displacement. It assumes a smooth two-dimensional velocity field and that each trajectory originates in one surface accumulation zone. The full-Stokes decomposition assumes isotropic scalar viscosity. It does not establish a universal mechanical bound on \(R_{\mathrm{FS}}\), because none follows from the Stokes equations alone. The localized-obstacle estimate depends on externally supplied bounds on minimum speed, deposition speed and reverse shear. The periodic cancellation result is first order and can be destroyed by asymmetry or nonlinear geometry.

Most importantly, the theorem diagnoses overturning of a continuous material isochrone. An observed age reversal can also result from faulting, unconformity, multiple source regions, freeze-on ice, sampling across a sharply dipping but discontinuous structure, or unresolved three-dimensional geometry. The order diagnostic should therefore be used with structural observations rather than as a stand-alone interpretation.

\section{Conclusions}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item A steady two-dimensional isochrone preserves depositional order if and only if its signed order Jacobian remains positive (cf. Equation~\eqref{eq:Jexact}). The equivalent observable condition is \(\tau_z<0\) (Equation~\eqref{eq:Jage}), and in the ordered regime \(\Jord\) is the reciprocal of the established vertical thinning function (Equation~\eqref{eq:thinningrelation}).

  \item Pointwise surface-fast shear, \(u_z\geq0\), is sufficient but not necessary. The exact condition is the cumulative order budget \(\Rneg<1+\Rpos\) (Equation~\eqref{eq:ordercondition}). Local extrusion changes stratigraphic order only if its weighted path integral removes the initial depositional contribution and all accumulated normal shear.

  \item Full-Stokes mechanics does not guarantee this condition for arbitrary glacier geometry. Gravity-driven shear favors order, but longitudinal stress, nonhydrostatic pressure and horizontal gradients of vertical velocity can generate reverse shear (cf. Equation~\eqref{eq:uzdecomp}). Stagnation and recirculation invalidate the one-way coordinate construction entirely.

  \item The shallow-ice regime preserves order (Equation~\eqref{eq:SIAorder}). A non-stagnant localized obstacle obeys the speed-and-shear bound in Equation~\eqref{eq:obstaclebound}, and small-amplitude periodic roughness about a uniform plug-flow background has no first-order net order change over a complete wavelength (Equation~\eqref{eq:periodiccancel}).

  \item The idealized full-Stokes examples generate recumbent isochrones directly from ordered surface-deposition maps (Figures~\ref{fig:numericalfolds}--\ref{fig:numericalconvergence}). One crosses the threshold through persistent reverse shear while horizontal speed remains comparable to its deposition value. The other combines reverse shear with slow-flow amplification. Both retain \(u>0\), and both retain a negative neighboring-trajectory Jacobian under mesh refinement.

  \item The intuition that fixed obstacles produce draping rather than folds is conditional. It holds in ordinary fast, shallow throughflow and in the bounded regimes considered above. Outside those regimes, a steady full-Stokes velocity field can generate a fold without an inherited perturbation if the cumulative diagnostic in Equation~\eqref{eq:Jdiagnostic} reaches zero.

  \item For NSF COLDEX at Allan Hills, the next calculation is to integrate \(\Rpos\), \(\Rneg\) and \(\Jord\) along reconstructed source-to-core trajectories using Equations~\eqref{eq:trajectory}--\eqref{eq:Jdiagnostic}. A three-dimensional material map is required where cross-flow or multiple source regions are important. The same diagnostic can test stratigraphic order at continuous old-ice sites such as Beyond EPICA.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Inverse-coordinate identities}
\label{app:inverse}

Equation~\eqref{eq:agepsiJacobian} can be written
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix}
\tau_x & \tau_z\\
\psi_x & \psi_z
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
\dd x\\
\dd z
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\dd\tau\\
\dd\psi
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\det=1.
\end{equation}
The inverse transformation is
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix}
\dd x\\
\dd z
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\psi_z & -\tau_z\\
-\psi_x & \tau_x
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
\dd\tau\\
\dd\psi
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
 u & -\tau_z\\
 w & \tau_x
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
\dd\tau\\
\dd\psi
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:inversemap}
\end{equation}
Therefore
\begin{equation}
 x_{\tau}|_{\psi}=u,
 \quad
 z_{\tau}|_{\psi}=w,
 \quad
 x_{\psi}|_{\tau}=-\tau_z,
 \quad
 z_{\psi}|_{\tau}=\tau_x.
\end{equation}
The first two identities state that advancing one unit in age at fixed streamline follows the velocity for one unit of time. The latter two specify the equal-age tangent. Multiplication by \(\dd\psi/\dd x_0=\dot a_0\) yields Equation~\eqref{eq:Jage} and the corresponding vertical tangent component.

\section{Extension to steady variable-density flow}
\label{app:density}

Below the firn, glacier ice is accurately treated as incompressible with nearly constant density. The coordinate construction extends to a steady variable-density flow satisfying
\begin{equation}
\nabla\cdot(\rho\uvec)=0.
\end{equation}
Define the mass-flux streamfunction by
\begin{equation}
\rho u=\psi_z,
\qquad
\rho w=-\psi_x.
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
\begin{vmatrix}
\tau_x & \tau_z \\
\psi_x & \psi_z
\end{vmatrix}
=\rho(u\tau_x+w\tau_z)=\rho.
\end{equation}
The mapping remains nonsingular for \(\rho>0\), and the same order interpretation follows after replacing volume flux by mass flux. The constant-density formula used in the main text is therefore not conceptually tied to strict incompressibility, although the weights in the cumulative integral acquire density factors.

\section{First-order periodic cancellation about plug flow}
\label{app:periodic}

Let
\begin{equation}
 u(x,z;\epsilon_b)=U_b+\epsilon_b u_1(x,z)+O(\epsilon_b^2),
 \qquad U_b>0,
\end{equation}
where \(U_b\) is spatially uniform and \(u_1\) is periodic in \(x\) with wavelength \(2\pi/k\) and zero horizontal mean at each elevation. Then
\begin{equation}
 u_z=\epsilon_b u_{1,z}+O(\epsilon_b^2),
 \qquad
 u^{-3}=U_b^{-3}+O(\epsilon_b).
\end{equation}
Because the background integrand is identically zero, the \(O(\epsilon_b)\) displacement of the trajectory affects the integral only at \(O(\epsilon_b^2)\). Evaluating the first-order term along the unperturbed streamline \(z=z_0\) gives
\begin{equation}
\int_x^{x+2\pi/k}\frac{u_z}{u^3}\,\dd\xi
=\frac{\epsilon_b}{U_b^3}
\int_x^{x+2\pi/k}u_{1,z}(\xi,z_0)\,\dd\xi
+O(\epsilon_b^2)
=O(\epsilon_b^2),
\end{equation}
because differentiation with respect to \(z\) preserves the zero horizontal mean of \(u_1\). This establishes Equation~\eqref{eq:periodiccancel}. For a vertically sheared background, trajectory displacement can act on the nonzero background integrand at first order, so the cancellation does not follow without additional assumptions.
