\large A cumulative reverse-shear criterion for full-Stokes flow}

\small Department of Earth and Space Sciences, University of Washington, Seattle, Washington, USA\\
\small Correspondence: \texttt{bpl7@uw.edu}}

\begin{abstract}
Ancient ice recovered by the Center for Oldest Ice Exploration from the Allan Hills extends into the Miocene, but steep layers, age reversals and small-scale folds complicate interpretation. Existing steady-flow theory shows that isochrones cannot overturn when horizontal speed increases upward. Full-Stokes flow over rough beds can nevertheless contain local extrusion, making that pointwise condition stronger than necessary. We derive a physical-coordinate signed order factor,
\[
\Jord=\frac{u}{u_{s0}}\left[1+\dot a_0u_{s0}\int_{x_0}^{x}\frac{u_z}{u^3}\,\dd\xi\right]=-\dot a_0\tau_z.
\]
Where layers remain ordered, \(\Jord\) is the reciprocal of the established vertical thinning function. Positive, zero and negative \(\Jord\) denote ordered, vertical and overturned stratigraphy. Splitting vertical shear into normal and reverse parts gives the necessary-and-sufficient condition \(\Rneg<1+\Rpos\): reverse shear must erase both depositional ordering and accumulated surface-fast shear. Full-Stokes mechanics gives no universal sign guarantee, although shallow-ice flow preserves order. We derive a full-Stokes residual threshold, sufficient bounds for non-stagnant localized obstacles, a first-order cancellation result for periodic roughness about plug flow and a trajectory diagnostic. The advance is an explicit cumulative shear budget and its mechanical consequences, rather than a new state variable or universal no-folding theorem.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} ice stratigraphy; recumbent folds; Allan Hills; COLDEX; full-Stokes flow; extrusion flow; age equation; blue ice

\section{Introduction}

The oldest ice can be the least stratigraphically simple. Antarctic blue-ice areas expose ancient glacier ice by combining surface mass loss with upward ice motion, thereby providing access to climatic intervals far older than those recovered from conventional vertical cores \parencite{Bintanja1999,Spaulding2012}. At the Allan Hills, shallow cores first yielded a stratigraphically disturbed section near \SI{1}{Ma} \parencite{Higgins2015}, then discontinuous atmospheric snapshots older than \SI{2}{Ma} \parencite{Yan2019}, and most recently Pliocene and Miocene ice and air with reported ages approaching \SI{6}{Ma} \parencite{Shackleton2025}. These discoveries are central to the Center for Oldest Ice Exploration (COLDEX) effort to reconstruct atmospheric composition and Antarctic climate beyond the continuous \SI{800}{ka} ice-core record.

The scientific opportunity is inseparable from a structural problem. Allan Hills ice is strongly thinned and locally out of order. The ALHIC1901 age--depth relation includes multiple reversals, while the deepest ice can represent very large time intervals per unit core length \parencite{Shackleton2025}. Three-dimensional electrical conductivity measurements resolve mean layer dips of approximately \(29^{\circ}\) and \(69^{\circ}\) in two recent Allan Hills cores \parencite{Kirkpatrick2025}. Microstructural observations from ALHIC1901 indicate pervasive simple shear, strongly elongated bubbles, crystal-orientation stripes, and centimetre-scale kink and z-folds \parencite{Stoll2026}. Radar and flow modelling independently show that the duration and continuity of the archive vary sharply across the blue-ice area and with basal geometry \parencite{Kehrl2018}. Deformation is therefore not a secondary correction to the paleoclimate record; it controls which ages are present, how they are ordered, and how much time a sample averages.

A basic unresolved question is whether a fixed bed obstacle in an otherwise steady glacier can create the finite-amplitude disturbance required for a recumbent fold. Flow over topography certainly bends internal layers. Full mechanical calculations predict draping over long-wavelength topography and overriding of shorter-wavelength relief \parencite{Hindmarsh2006}. Rough-bed Stokes solutions can also contain local extrusion, meaning that horizontal speed increases downward over part of the ice column \parencite{Gudmundsson1997linear,Waddington2010}. At larger bed slopes, separated secondary circulation is possible even in creeping flow \parencite{Gudmundsson1997nonlinear}. These results appear, at first sight, to undermine the common intuition that a steady obstacle can produce only a standing layer disturbance rather than a recumbent fold.

The apparent contradiction reflects three distinct concepts. A fixed obstacle can generate \emph{curvature}; local extrusion can rotate a material tangent in the sense favorable for overturning; and yet an isochrone can remain globally ordered. Recumbent folding requires a stronger event: particles deposited in an ordered sequence must reverse their horizontal order along an equal-age surface. Curvature, tangent rotation and order reversal are not equivalent.

The local theory of \textcite{Waddington2001}, hereafter WBA, formalized how a pre-existing wrinkle is flattened by longitudinal extension or overturned by shear. WBA also stated the key qualitative principle that layers spending their entire history in a steady-state flow field are not passively folded; a transient process must first create a disturbance relative to the steady stratigraphy. \textcite{JacobsonWaddington2004} replaced the local criterion with finite-strain integration along trajectories, and \textcite{JacobsonWaddington2005} showed that divide migration can place an inherited Raymond arch in a shear regime that turns it into a recumbent fold. For steady stratigraphy, \textcite{ParreninHindmarshRemy2006} introduced logarithmic flux coordinates for analytical age solutions, and \textcite{ParreninHindmarsh2007} generalized the construction to horizontally non-uniform velocity profiles. Their Appendix A showed that an isochrone cannot pass through vertical when horizontal velocity increases monotonically upward. The same characteristic framework subsequently underpinned numerical age solvers \parencite{HindmarshLeysingerVieliParrenin2009,ParreninChungMartin2025} and an explicit vertical thinning function \parencite{Parrenin2013,ParreninChungMartin2025}.

The monotonic-shear condition is powerful but unnecessarily restrictive. Full-Stokes glacier flow does not impose \(u_z\geq0\) pointwise. The correct question is therefore cumulative: how much reverse shear can a particle experience before the depositional ordering of the isochrone is lost? This paper answers that question for steady two-dimensional flow. We:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,label=(\roman*)]
  \item derive a physical-coordinate representation of the signed inverse vertical-thinning factor and its interpretation as horizontal order along an isochrone;
  \item express its sign as a cumulative competition between surface-fast and reverse vertical shear;
  \item decompose the reverse-shear budget using the full-Stokes stress balance;
  \item identify regimes in which ordinary glacier mechanics guarantees order and regimes in which it does not;
  \item derive a sufficient no-overturning bound for localized obstacles and a first-order cancellation result for periodic small-amplitude roughness; and
  \item formulate a direct trajectory diagnostic for full-Stokes models of Allan Hills and other old-ice sites.
\end{enumerate}

The novelty is deliberately narrow. The monotonic-shear no-overturning result is due to \textcite{ParreninHindmarsh2007}; the order factor derived below is the reciprocal of the vertical thinning function developed by \textcite{Parrenin2013} and implemented by \textcite{ParreninChungMartin2025}; the local wrinkle criterion and finite-strain interpretation are due to WBA and \textcite{JacobsonWaddington2004}; and rough-bed extrusion is established \parencite{Gudmundsson1997linear}. The contribution here is to expose the sign of the inverse thinning factor as an exact positive--negative \(u_z/u^3\) shear budget and to use that budget to obtain a full-Stokes residual threshold, localized-obstacle bounds, a restricted periodic-cancellation result and a direct trajectory diagnostic. These are reformulations and extensions of established steady-isocrone theory, not claims of a new universal state variable or no-folding theorem.

\section{Previous descriptions of stratigraphic folding}

\subsection{Passive and active folding}

Glacier stratification is a material structure produced at the surface and subsequently deformed by flow. Structural glaciology distinguishes passive deformation, in which layers are markers that do not alter the velocity field, from active folding, in which rheological or anisotropic contrasts feed back on stress and motion \parencite{Hudleston2015,JenningsHambrey2021}. The distinction matters because a prescribed smooth velocity field can amplify or overturn an existing perturbation without selecting its wavelength, whereas active deformation can generate disturbances and select spatial scales.

Microstructural folds in deep ice provide evidence for the active route. At GISP2, crystal-orientation stripes are associated with localized shear and grain-scale folding, and spatial variation in stripe density may create larger viscosity contrasts \parencite{Alley1997}. More recent numerical work shows that anisotropy and convergent flow can produce folds spanning scales from cloudy bands to radar reflectors \parencite{Bons2016,Bons2025,Zhang2024}. The analysis below concerns passive age transport in a prescribed velocity field. It supplies a necessary kinematic test for folding, not a dispersion relation for active instability.

\subsection{The WBA local wrinkle criterion}

Let \(m\) be the slope of an infinitesimal material-line segment in a two-dimensional velocity field \((u,w)\). WBA derived
\begin{equation}
\frac{\mathrm{D}m}{\mathrm{D}t}
= w_x+(w_z-u_x)m-u_zm^2.
\label{eq:wba}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:wba} is the Riccati form of the material-line equation and is standard continuum kinematics. WBA's distinctive step was to compare the rotation of a disturbed tangent with the rotation of the local steady isochrone tangent \(m_0\). A second equal-rotation direction separates disturbances that initially flatten toward \(m_0\) from those that rotate away from it. Their shear number expresses the competition between shear-driven rotation and longitudinal extension.

Equation~\eqref{eq:wba} is local. Applied pointwise along a complete curve, it contains the exact tangent kinematics of that curve. The published WBA stability diagnostic, however, does not by itself determine whether an entire equal-age surface loses its horizontal ordering. WBA emphasized this limitation over rough topography: a disturbed tangent can open relative to the local steady isochrone on the lee side of a bump while merely recovering deformation accumulated on the stoss side \parencite{Waddington2001}.

\subsection{Finite strain and steady isochrone geometry}

\textcite{JacobsonWaddington2004} integrated finite deformation along particle paths rather than relying on a homogeneous instantaneous velocity gradient. This is the appropriate framework for deciding whether an inherited open fold becomes recumbent after a long and spatially variable strain history. Their later divide-migration calculation supplied a concrete source of the inherited perturbation \parencite{JacobsonWaddington2005}.

A separate line of analysis concerns the geometry of isochrones generated continuously in a steady flow. \textcite{ParreninHindmarshRemy2006} introduced logarithmic total-flux and normalized-streamfunction coordinates in which particle trajectories are straight characteristics when the vertical velocity shape is horizontally uniform. \textcite{Hindmarsh2006} compared streamlines and isochrones over basal topography and showed that they are close when
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dot a}{U}\frac{L}{H}\ll1,
\label{eq:hindmarshscale}
\end{equation}
where \(\dot a\) is accumulation, \(U\) a horizontal speed, \(L\) the forcing wavelength and \(H\) the ice thickness. \textcite{ParreninHindmarsh2007} generalized the characteristic analysis to horizontally non-uniform velocity profiles and wrote isochrone slope as an instantaneous normalized-streamfunction contribution plus a path-history term. In their notation, the sign of \(1-\alpha\) determines whether neighboring particles preserve their order, and \(\alpha>1\) corresponds to an isochrone that has passed through vertical. They proved that this cannot occur if horizontal velocity increases upward. \textcite{HindmarshLeysingerVieliParrenin2009} used these solutions to verify a large-scale numerical age model, and \textcite{ParreninChungMartin2025} recently implemented the same flux-coordinate framework for age, particle origin and vertical thinning.

\section{Steady age transport and the order Jacobian}

\subsection{Setting and assumptions}

Consider a steady two-dimensional glacier velocity
\begin{equation}
\uvec(x,z)=(u(x,z),w(x,z))
\end{equation}
in a domain bounded above by the steady surface \(z=s(x)\). The kinematic result requires only the following assumptions on the region and trajectories of interest:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,label=(A\arabic*)]
  \item The velocity is continuously differentiable and incompressible,
  \begin{equation}
  u_x+w_z=0.
  \label{eq:incompressible}
  \end{equation}
  \item Horizontal motion is one-way,
  \begin{equation}
  u(x,z)>0,
  \label{eq:oneway}
  \end{equation}
  after orienting \(x\) downstream.
  \item Every trajectory considered enters the ice through a unique accumulating branch of the surface. The surface accumulation rate, expressed in vertical ice-equivalent units, satisfies \(\dot a(x)>0\) on that branch.
  \item Stratigraphic age is a passive, non-diffusive scalar. Its steady value \(\tau(x,z)\) satisfies
  \begin{equation}
  u\tau_x+w\tau_z=1,
  \qquad \tau=0\quad\text{at deposition}.
  \label{eq:age}
  \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

An impermeable bed is not required for the local result. Basal melting may terminate some trajectories without altering the derivation before termination. Basal freezing, in contrast, introduces a second material source and requires separate origin labels. Assumption (A2) excludes horizontal stagnation and closed circulation. Assumption (A3) requires special care in a blue-ice area because the local surface is an outlet; the material origin must instead be traced to an upstream inflow branch or another source.

Because of Equation~\eqref{eq:incompressible}, there is a streamfunction \(\psi\) such that
\begin{equation}
 u=\psi_z,
 \qquad
 w=-\psi_x.
 \label{eq:streamfunction}
\end{equation}
Combining Equations~\eqref{eq:age} and \eqref{eq:streamfunction} gives the exact Jacobian identity
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial(\tau,\psi)}{\partial(x,z)}
=\tau_x\psi_z-\tau_z\psi_x
=u\tau_x+w\tau_z
=1.
\label{eq:agepsiJacobian}
\end{equation}
Thus age and streamfunction form local area-preserving coordinates. Isochrones and streamlines intersect transversely wherever the assumptions hold. This is a classical flow-coordinate construction; it is useful here because its inverse directly measures stratigraphic order.

\subsection{Surface origin and travel time}

The streamfunction restricted to the surface is
\begin{equation}
Q(x)=\psi(x,s(x)).
\end{equation}
The steady surface kinematic condition is
\begin{equation}
 u_s s_x-w_s=\dot a,
\label{eq:surfacekinematic}
\end{equation}
where the subscript \(s\) denotes evaluation at the surface. Therefore
\begin{equation}
Q'(x)=\psi_x+\psi_zs_x=-w_s+u_ss_x=\dot a(x)>0.
\label{eq:Qprime}
\end{equation}
Every streamline label \(\psi\) has a unique deposition position \(x_0(\psi)\) satisfying
\begin{equation}
Q(x_0)=\psi,
\qquad
\frac{\dd x_0}{\dd\psi}=\frac{1}{\dot a_0},
\label{eq:x0psi}
\end{equation}
where \(\dot a_0=\dot a(x_0)\).

At fixed \(\psi\), Equation~\eqref{eq:age} gives
\begin{equation}
\left.\frac{\partial\tau}{\partial x}\right|_{\psi}=\frac{1}{u}.
\end{equation}
Consequently the age at \((x,\psi)\) is the travel time from deposition,
\begin{equation}
\tau(x,\psi)=\int_{x_0(\psi)}^{x}
\frac{\dd\xi}{u\bigl(\xi,z(\xi,\psi)\bigr)}.
\label{eq:traveltime}
\end{equation}
At fixed \(x\), Equation~\eqref{eq:streamfunction} implies
\begin{equation}
 z_{\psi}=\frac{1}{u}.
\label{eq:zpsi}
\end{equation}
Differentiating Equation~\eqref{eq:traveltime} with respect to \(\psi\), using Equations~\eqref{eq:x0psi} and \eqref{eq:zpsi}, yields
\begin{equation}
\tau_{\psi}
=-\frac{1}{\dot a_0u_{s0}}
-\int_{x_0}^{x}\frac{u_z}{u^3}\,\dd\xi,
\label{eq:taupsi}
\end{equation}
where \(u_{s0}=u(x_0,s(x_0))\). The first term records the separation imposed at deposition; the integral records the subsequent differential travel time between neighboring streamlines.

\subsection{Exact order theorem}

Parameterize an isochrone of fixed age by the surface deposition coordinate \(x_0\). The relevant quantity is not its curvature but the horizontal projection of its tangent.

\begin{definition}[Isochrone-order Jacobian]
The isochrone-order Jacobian is
\begin{equation}
\Jord(x,x_0)
=\left.\frac{\partial x}{\partial x_0}\right|_{\tau}.
\label{eq:Jdefinition}
\end{equation}
It measures whether particles deposited in increasing order of \(x_0\) remain in increasing downstream order on an equal-age surface.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Cumulative order criterion]
Under assumptions (A1)--(A4), the isochrone-order Jacobian is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\Jord
=\frac{u(x,z)}{u_{s0}}
\left[
1+\dot a_0u_{s0}
\int_{x_0}^{x}\frac{u_z}{u^3}\,\dd\xi
\right].
}
\label{eq:Jexact}
\end{equation}
It is also related exactly to the vertical age gradient by
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\Jord=-\dot a_0\tau_z.}
\label{eq:Jage}
\end{equation}
Hence \(\Jord>0\) denotes ordered stratigraphy, \(\Jord=0\) a vertical tangent, and \(\Jord<0\) an overturned limb.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
At fixed age, differentiation of \(\tau(x,\psi)\) gives
\begin{equation}
\left.\frac{\partial x}{\partial\psi}\right|_{\tau}
=-\frac{\tau_{\psi}}{\tau_x|_{\psi}}
=-u\tau_{\psi}.
\end{equation}
Multiplication by \(\dd\psi/\dd x_0=\dot a_0\) and substitution of Equation~\eqref{eq:taupsi} gives Equation~\eqref{eq:Jexact}. At fixed \(x\), \(\tau_z=\tau_{\psi}\psi_z=u\tau_{\psi}\), which gives Equation~\eqref{eq:Jage}.
\end{proof}

The vertical thinning function \(\lambda_z\) is conventionally the present vertical thickness of an infinitesimal age layer divided by its ice-equivalent thickness at deposition. For an ordered layer, a positive age increment \(\dd\tau\) corresponds to the present vertical thickness \(-\dd z=-\dd\tau/\tau_z\) and the initial thickness \(\dot a_0\dd\tau\). Hence
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\lambda_z
=-\frac{1}{\dot a_0\tau_z}
=\Jord^{-1},
\qquad \Jord>0.
}
\label{eq:thinningrelation}
\end{equation}
Thus \(\Jord\) is not a new independent state variable: in the ordered regime it is the inverse of the established vertical thinning function \parencite{Parrenin2013,ParreninChungMartin2025}. Equation~\eqref{eq:Jage} provides its signed continuation through a vertical tangent. The standard positive thinning function diverges as \(\tau_z\rightarrow0\), whereas \(\Jord\) crosses zero regularly and records orientation reversal.

At deposition, \(x=x_0\), \(u=u_{s0}\), and \(\Jord=1\). At the first vertical tangent, \(\tau_z=0\), but the material curve remains regular. Inverting Equation~\eqref{eq:agepsiJacobian} shows that
\begin{equation}
\left.\frac{\partial z}{\partial x_0}\right|_{\tau}
=\dot a_0\tau_x.
\end{equation}
When \(\Jord=0\), Equation~\eqref{eq:age} gives \(\tau_x=1/u\), and therefore
\begin{equation}
\left.\frac{\partial z}{\partial x_0}\right|_{\tau,\Jord=0}
=\frac{\dot a_0}{u}>0.
\end{equation}
The divergence of the conventional slope \(m=\dd z/\dd x\) at overturning is therefore a coordinate singularity, not a singularity of the layer itself.

\subsection{Positive and reverse shear budgets}

Define
\begin{equation}
 u_z^+=\max(u_z,0),
 \qquad
 u_z^-=\max(-u_z,0),
\end{equation}
and the dimensionless cumulative contributions
\begin{align}
\Rpos(x,x_0)
&=\dot a_0u_{s0}
\int_{x_0}^{x}\frac{u_z^+}{u^3}\,\dd\xi,
\label{eq:Pdef}\\
\Rneg(x,x_0)
&=\dot a_0u_{s0}
\int_{x_0}^{x}\frac{u_z^-}{u^3}\,\dd\xi.
\label{eq:Rdef}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:Jexact} becomes
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\Jord=\frac{u}{u_{s0}}\left(1+\Rpos-\Rneg\right).
}
\label{eq:JPR}
\end{equation}
Because \(u/u_{s0}>0\), the necessary-and-sufficient condition for preservation of horizontal order is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\Rneg<1+\Rpos.}
\label{eq:ordercondition}
\end{equation}
The first vertical tangent occurs at
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\Rneg=1+\Rpos,}
\label{eq:verticalcondition}
\end{equation}
and overturning occurs when \(\Rneg>1+\Rpos\).

The unit term in Equations~\eqref{eq:JPR}--\eqref{eq:verticalcondition} is the finite initial ordering imposed at deposition. Positive \(u_z\), the usual surface-fast glacier profile, strengthens that ordering. Reverse shear must overcome both. Thus a local region with \(u_z<0\) is not itself a fold criterion.

In travel-time coordinates, \(\dd x=u\,\dd t\), so
\begin{equation}
\Rneg
=\dot a_0u_{s0}
\int_0^T\frac{u_z^-}{u^2}\,\dd t.
\label{eq:Rtime}
\end{equation}
Reverse shear is amplified by residence time and by slow horizontal motion. This weighting explains why local extrusion in fast throughflow may be harmless, while modest reverse shear near stagnation can dominate the order budget.

\begin{corollary}[Monotonic-shear result]
If \(u_z\geq0\) everywhere along a trajectory, then \(\Rneg=0\), \(\Jord>0\), and the steady isochrone cannot overturn.
\end{corollary}

This corollary is the physical-coordinate statement of the no-overturning result in Appendix A of \textcite{ParreninHindmarsh2007}. It is sufficient, not necessary. Equation~\eqref{eq:ordercondition} permits finite intervals of reverse shear provided their cumulative weighted contribution remains subcritical.

\subsection{Relation to the Parrenin--Hindmarsh path factor}

\textcite{ParreninHindmarsh2007} used a normalized streamfunction and logarithmic flux coordinates. For two neighboring particles, their Appendix A gives a mapping of the form
\begin{equation}
\delta\pi=(1-\alpha)\,\delta\pi_0,
\end{equation}
where \(\alpha>1\) corresponds to an isochrone exceeding vertical. Their present and surface-origin coordinates are monotone functions of \(x\) and \(x_0\) wherever assumptions (A2)--(A3) hold. Consequently,
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{sgn}(1-\alpha)
=\operatorname{sgn}\left(
\left.\frac{\partial x}{\partial x_0}\right|_{\tau}
\right)
=\operatorname{sgn}(\Jord)
=\operatorname{sgn}(-\tau_z).
\label{eq:PHsign}
\end{equation}
The present result is therefore not an independent no-overturning theorem. Nor is \(\Jord\) independent of the established thinning variable: Equation~\eqref{eq:thinningrelation} identifies it as the signed inverse vertical-thinning factor. The contribution of the physical-space representation is that it exposes the positive and negative parts of vertical shear, remains regular at the first vertical tangent and can be evaluated directly from a numerical velocity field. The current \texttt{age\_flow\_line} formulation assumes strictly positive accumulation for its horizontal flux coordinate and no reverse horizontal flow for its vertical flux coordinate; it computes age and positive vertical thinning within that admissible regime \parencite{ParreninChungMartin2025}.

\section{What does full-Stokes mechanics imply?}

\subsection{Exact stress decomposition of vertical shear}

The order theorem is kinematic. We now ask whether ordinary glacier mechanics forces Equation~\eqref{eq:ordercondition} to hold. Consider steady, isothermal or thermomechanically prescribed, isotropic generalized-Newtonian Stokes flow,
\begin{align}
\nabla\cdot\uvec&=0,
\label{eq:mass}\\
-\nabla p+\nabla\cdot\taut+\rho\bm{g}&=\bm{0},
\label{eq:momentum}\\
\taut&=2\eta\Dten,
\qquad
\Dten=\tfrac12\left(\nabla\uvec+\nabla\uvec^{\mathsf T}\right),
\qquad \eta>0.
\label{eq:rheology}
\end{align}
The scalar viscosity \(\eta\) may depend on temperature and effective strain rate. Anisotropic rheology is discussed separately below.

With \(z\) vertical and gravity having no horizontal component, the horizontal momentum equation is
\begin{equation}
-p_x+\tau_{xx,x}+\tau_{xz,z}=0.
\label{eq:xmomentum}
\end{equation}
Write
\begin{equation}
p=p_{\mathrm{atm}}+\rho g(s-z)+p_{\mathrm{nh}},
\end{equation}
where \(p_{\mathrm{nh}}\) contains all nonhydrostatic pressure, including the finite-slope surface correction. Integrating Equation~\eqref{eq:xmomentum} downward from the free surface gives
\begin{equation}
\tau_{xz}(x,z)
=\tau_{xz}^{s}
-\rho g s_x(s-z)
+\int_z^s\left(\tau_{xx,x}-p_{\mathrm{nh},x}\right)\,\dd\zeta.
\label{eq:tauxzint}
\end{equation}
Because \(\tau_{xz}=\eta(u_z+w_x)\),
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
 u_z
 =\underbrace{\frac{\rho g(s-z)(-s_x)}{\eta}}_{G}
 +\underbrace{
 \left[
 -w_x+\frac{\tau_{xz}^{s}}{\eta}
 +\frac{1}{\eta}\int_z^s
 \left(\tau_{xx,x}-p_{\mathrm{nh},x}\right)\,\dd\zeta
 \right]}_{R_{\mathrm{FS}}}.
}
\label{eq:uzdecomp}
\end{equation}
For a surface descending downstream, \(s_x\leq0\), the gravity-driven term \(G\) is nonnegative and favors order preservation. The residual \(R_{\mathrm{FS}}\) contains the full-Stokes effects omitted from the shallow-ice shear balance: gradients of vertical velocity, longitudinal stress, nonhydrostatic pressure and the Cartesian surface shear-stress component.

Substitution into Equation~\eqref{eq:Jexact} gives
\begin{equation}
\Jord=\frac{u}{u_{s0}}
\left(1+\Ggrav+\Gfs\right),
\label{eq:Jfullstokes}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{align}
\Ggrav&=\dot a_0u_{s0}
\int_{x_0}^{x}\frac{G}{u^3}\,\dd\xi\geq0,
\label{eq:Ggrav}\\
\Gfs&=\dot a_0u_{s0}
\int_{x_0}^{x}\frac{R_{\mathrm{FS}}}{u^3}\,\dd\xi.
\label{eq:Gfs}
\end{align}
The exact full-Stokes failure condition is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\Gfs\leq-\left(1+\Ggrav\right).
}
\label{eq:FSfailure}
\end{equation}
A conservative sufficient condition for no overturning is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\dot a_0u_{s0}
\int_{x_0}^{x}
\frac{\negpart{R_{\mathrm{FS}}}}{u^3}\,\dd\xi<1.
}
\label{eq:FSsufficient}
\end{equation}
This inequality discards all positive contributions, including gravity-driven shear, and is therefore stronger than necessary but easy to test.

\subsection{There is no universal full-Stokes sign theorem}

Neither incompressibility nor the Stokes equations fixes the sign of \(u_z\). The constitutive relation constrains \(u_z+w_x\), while longitudinal stress and nonhydrostatic pressure can alter the Cartesian vertical gradient of horizontal speed. Analytical rough-bed solutions demonstrate local velocity maxima and minima, and therefore regions with \(u_z<0\), even for gravity-driven linear viscous flow \parencite{Gudmundsson1997linear}. The historical extrusion-flow literature likewise shows that surface-fast velocity profiles are not a pointwise theorem of full-Stokes mechanics \parencite{Waddington2010}.

At stronger roughness, the one-way-flow assumption itself can fail. \textcite{Gudmundsson1997nonlinear} found separated secondary circulation in troughs of sufficiently steep sinusoidal beds. Ideal no-slip Stokes flow near a sharp corner can contain nested Moffatt eddies \parencite{Moffatt1964}. These examples are not direct models of Allan Hills: glacier beds may slide, Glen flow is nonlinear, and real corners are rounded. Their theoretical significance is that a steady creeping-flow equation does not forbid recirculation. When \(u=0\) or trajectories close, the downstream travel-time coordinate and unique surface-origin map cease to exist, so Equations~\eqref{eq:Jexact}--\eqref{eq:ordercondition} no longer apply globally.

The distinction between local extrusion and cumulative order reversal is central. Published rough-bed solutions establish that \(u_z\) can be negative. They do not, by that fact alone, establish that \(\Rneg\) reaches \(1+\Rpos\). The latter is an order-one integrated threshold.

\section{Regimes that preserve order}

\subsection{Shallow-ice flow}

Under the conventional shallow-ice approximation,
\begin{equation}
\tau_{xz}\simeq-\rho g(s-z)s_x.
\label{eq:SIAstress}
\end{equation}
For downstream flow with \(s_x<0\), \(\tau_{xz}>0\). An isotropic Glen-type rheology gives \(u_z\) with the same sign as \(\tau_{xz}\), while leading-order basal sliding contributes a depth-independent horizontal speed \parencite{CuffeyPaterson2010}. Therefore
\begin{equation}
 u_z\geq0,
 \qquad \Rneg=0,
 \qquad \Jord>0.
\label{eq:SIAorder}
\end{equation}
A fixed bed obstacle can produce arches, troughs and finite curvature in shallow-ice stratigraphy, but it cannot overturn a steady isochrone under these assumptions.

\subsection{Regular thin-film full-Stokes flow}

Let
\begin{equation}
\varepsilon=\frac{H}{L}\ll1
\end{equation}
be the aspect ratio of a smooth glacier. Higher-order glacier models reproduce the Stokes solution to second order in \(\varepsilon\) under their regular asymptotic assumptions, including a range of basal slip regimes \parencite{Blatter1995,SchoofHindmarsh2010}. Suppose the non-shallow contribution scales as
\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{FS}}=O\left(\varepsilon^2\frac{U}{H}\right)
\label{eq:RFSscale}
\end{equation}
through a region of length \(L=H/\varepsilon\), and suppose \(u\) remains comparable to \(U\). Then
\begin{equation}
\Gfs=O\left(\frac{\dot a}{U}\varepsilon\right).
\label{eq:GFSscale}
\end{equation}
Thus small regular full-Stokes corrections cannot erase the unit depositional margin when
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dot a}{U}\varepsilon\ll1.
\label{eq:thinorder}
\end{equation}
This is an asymptotic sufficient regime, not a uniform theorem. It can fail near divides, abrupt sliding transitions, contact zones, steep obstacles, very slow flow or other regions where the thin-film expansion is singular or nonuniform.

\subsection{A sufficient bound for a localized obstacle}

The cumulative criterion yields a direct obstacle bound without requiring pointwise monotonic shear.

\begin{proposition}[Localized reverse-shear bound]
Suppose reverse shear is confined to a trajectory segment of horizontal length \(\ell_-\). On that segment, assume
\begin{equation}
 u\geq rU,
 \qquad
 u_{s0}\leq r_sU,
 \qquad
 u_z^-\leq C\frac{U}{d},
\label{eq:boundassumptions}
\end{equation}
where \(U>0\) is a representative speed, \(d\) is the vertical deformation scale, \(r>0\), \(r_s>0\), and \(C>0\). Then
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\Rneg\leq
C\frac{r_s}{r^3}
\frac{\dot a_0}{U}
\frac{\ell_-}{d}.
}
\label{eq:obstaclebound}
\end{equation}
A sufficient condition for no overturning is that the right-hand side be less than one.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Substitute Equation~\eqref{eq:boundassumptions} into Equation~\eqref{eq:Rdef} and bound the integral by its maximum integrand multiplied by \(\ell_-\).
\end{proof}

For an isolated obstacle whose horizontal and vertical influence scales are both \(O(H)\), with non-stagnant throughflow,
\begin{equation}
\Rneg=O\left(\frac{\dot a}{U}\right).
\label{eq:isolatedscale}
\end{equation}
In a typical interior or flank setting where \(U\gg\dot a\), an isolated fixed bump can create local extrusion and visible draping but is far from the order-one reverse-shear dose required for overturning. Conversely, a long reverse-shear region with \(d\sim H\) approaches the threshold only when
\begin{equation}
\frac{\ell_-}{H}
\gtrsim
\frac{U}{\dot a_0}\frac{r^3}{Cr_s},
\label{eq:lengththreshold}
\end{equation}
unless the flow becomes slow enough for the \(r^{-3}\) factor to dominate.

Equation~\eqref{eq:obstaclebound} makes precise why ``flow over a bump cannot fold an isochrone'' is often a good glacier approximation but not a universal Stokes theorem. The approximation depends on non-stagnant flow, a localized deformation zone and a small accumulation-to-speed ratio.

\subsection{First-order cancellation over periodic roughness about plug flow}

A second reason that fixed roughness may have weak cumulative effect is cancellation, but the clean result requires a plug-flow background. Let
\begin{equation}
 u(x,z;\epsilon_b)=U_b+\epsilon_b u_1(x,z)+O(\epsilon_b^2),
 \qquad U_b>0,
\label{eq:plugperturbation}
\end{equation}
where \(U_b\) is spatially uniform and \(u_1\) is periodic in \(x\), with wavelength \(2\pi/k\) and zero horizontal mean at each elevation. Along the unperturbed trajectory \(z=z_0\),
\begin{equation}
\frac{u_z}{u^3}
=\epsilon_b\frac{u_{1,z}(x,z_0)}{U_b^3}+O(\epsilon_b^2).
\label{eq:periodicshear}
\end{equation}
Therefore, over one complete wavelength,
\begin{equation}
\int_{x}^{x+2\pi/k}\frac{u_z}{u^3}\,\dd\xi=O(\epsilon_b^2).
\label{eq:periodiccancel}
\end{equation}
The perturbative frictionless rough-bed solution of \textcite{Gudmundsson1997linear} has this plug-flow structure: local extrusion occurs, but its first-order stoss- and lee-side contributions cancel over a complete symmetric wavelength.

Cancellation is not guaranteed for a sheared background, a solitary asymmetric obstacle, a finite path ending mid-cycle, strongly nonlinear roughness or near-stagnant trajectories. Equation~\eqref{eq:periodiccancel} is therefore a restricted asymptotic result, not a general symmetry theorem.

\section{How the order theorem can fail}

\subsection{Cumulative extrusion without stagnation}

The most direct steady-flow route is Equation~\eqref{eq:verticalcondition}. A glacier with \(u>0\) can overturn a steady isochrone if reverse shear is sufficiently strong, sufficiently persistent or sufficiently concentrated where \(u\) is small. The exact threshold is
\begin{equation}
\dot a_0u_{s0}
\int_{x_0}^{x}\frac{u_z^-}{u^3}\,\dd\xi
=
1+
\dot a_0u_{s0}
\int_{x_0}^{x}\frac{u_z^+}{u^3}\,\dd\xi.
\label{eq:exactthresholdexpanded}
\end{equation}
No change in boundary conditions is logically necessary if a steady full-Stokes velocity field satisfies this equality. The existing extrusion-flow literature establishes plausible ingredients but, to our knowledge, has not evaluated this cumulative isochrone-order threshold.

\subsection{Stagnation, separation and closed circulation}

If \(u\) reaches zero, the \(x\)-travel-time representation becomes singular. If a secondary circulation forms, a streamline may not have a unique surface origin and may intersect an equal-age surface in a topology not represented by a single downstream coordinate. Strong roughness, cavities and sharp corners are therefore not merely large values of \(\Rneg\); they can invalidate the coordinate system on which the theorem rests. Numerical calculations should treat \(u=0\) as an event and switch to a more general Lagrangian deformation analysis.

\subsection{Surface ablation and sublimation}

An accumulating surface is an inflow boundary on which newly deposited material has \(\tau=0\). An ablating surface is an outflow boundary at which existing isochrones terminate. In a blue-ice area, the surface streamfunction need not be globally monotone because
\begin{equation}
Q'(x)=\dot a(x)
\end{equation}
changes sign between accumulation and ablation zones. A streamline may enter through an upstream accumulation region and leave through a downstream ablation region. The theorem can still be applied to an interior trajectory if that trajectory has a unique origin on an upstream accumulating branch, but \(Q^{-1}\) must be constructed on that branch rather than on the entire local surface. Multiple inflow branches or an origin on basal freeze-on require a more general material-source label.

Steady ablation most naturally produces upward-bent and truncated layers, not an intact fold. However, migration, onset or cessation of an ablation zone changes the age field relative to the new steady solution and supplies precisely the inherited disturbance required by WBA. Surface sublimation followed by burial can also preserve an unconformity with steep or truncated limbs. At Allan Hills, the present surface is an ablation boundary and therefore lies outside the simplest theorem even if the subsurface velocity is steady.

\subsection{Basal melting and freeze-on}

Basal melting removes trajectories and truncates layers. Basal freeze-on introduces ice through a second inflow boundary, so one surface deposition coordinate cannot label the entire stratigraphy. Numerical models show that freeze-on can generate plume-like structures and complex internal layering extending through a large fraction of the ice thickness \parencite{LeysingerVieli2018}. A moving melt/freeze boundary additionally makes the velocity and age problem time dependent.

\subsection{Time-dependent geometry and basal traction}

If the bed, free surface, divide position or basal traction changes, the inherited age field is not the steady age solution of the new velocity field. The perturbation then obeys a homogeneous advection equation only after the change; it need not vanish initially. Divide migration converts a formerly steady Raymond arch into a fold seed \parencite{JacobsonWaddington2005}. Traveling slippery or sticky patches produce moving-frame critical layers and thickness-scale overturning \parencite{Wolovick2014,WolovickCreyts2016}. Bedrock uplift, rotation or erosion similarly changes the reference stratigraphy and can inject a finite-amplitude disturbance even when subsequent flow is steady.

\subsection{Active rheology, anisotropy and three-dimensional flow}

Equation~\eqref{eq:uzdecomp} assumes isotropic scalar viscosity. Crystal fabric, impurities, grain size and damage can alter both the stress--strain relation and the velocity field in response to stratigraphy. Full-Stokes anisotropic divide models already demonstrate substantial changes in isochrone shape and age--depth relations \parencite{MartinGudmundssonPritchardGagliardini2009}. More strongly coupled heterogeneity can generate folds internally rather than merely advecting an imposed perturbation \parencite{Alley1997,ThorsteinssonWaddington2002,Bons2016,Bons2025}. The Allan Hills microstructure indicates that anisotropy and localized shear cannot be ignored \parencite{Stoll2026}.

The order theorem is also intrinsically two-dimensional. In three dimensions, incompressibility provides a vector potential rather than a scalar streamfunction, and neighboring surface origins can exchange order in one horizontal projection while remaining separated in the other. Divide models show that along-ridge flow and divide migration alter isochrone architecture even when cross-flow is modest \parencite{MartinHindmarshNavarro2009}. A three-dimensional generalization should track the two-dimensional Jacobian mapping a surface deposition patch to an equal-age surface. The scalar \(\Jord\) is recovered only when cross-flow is negligible and a coherent flow tube exists.

\section{Implications for Allan Hills and COLDEX}

The Allan Hills setting sits close to several limits in which a fixed-obstacle argument becomes unreliable. Present-day horizontal motion near ALHIC1901 is only centimetres per year, the ice rises toward an ablating surface, and the bed contains steep buried nunatak geometry \parencite{Spaulding2012,Kehrl2018,Stoll2026}. Slow motion enhances the \(u^{-3}\) weighting in Equation~\eqref{eq:Rdef}. The local ablation surface is an outlet rather than the deposition boundary, so relevant source-to-core trajectories must be traced to an upstream accumulation branch or another material source. Strong relief raises the possibility of nonhydrostatic stress, local extrusion and near-stagnant flow. The small parameter \(\dot a/U\) that protects ordinary fast flank flow may therefore not be small along the relevant source-to-core trajectories.

At the same time, the observed deformation cannot be attributed to present-day steady geometry without a trajectory calculation. Steep dip is not overturning: the \(69^{\circ}\) mean dip in ALHIC2302 remains below vertical \parencite{Kirkpatrick2025}. Centimetre-scale z-folds in ALHIC1901 demonstrate local folding but are too small by themselves to explain all core-scale age reversals \parencite{Stoll2026}. The inverted age--depth relation in ALHIC1901 is the more direct evidence that \(\tau_z\) has changed sign along at least some sampling intervals \parencite{Shackleton2025}. Equation~\eqref{eq:Jage} translates that observation into \(\Jord<0\) if the sampled layers belong to a single surface-origin family; alternatively, multiple source boundaries, unconformities or faults could also generate apparent age reversal.

The theory suggests a falsifiable hierarchy of hypotheses.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,label=(\arabic*)]
  \item \textbf{Steady fixed geometry.} Compute the present or reconstructed steady full-Stokes field and evaluate \(\Rpos\), \(\Rneg\) and \(\Jord\) along source-to-core trajectories. If \(1+\Rpos-\Rneg\) remains positive with a substantial margin, fixed present-day geometry cannot produce the observed recumbent folding.
  \item \textbf{Steady but singular or multi-source flow.} Test for stagnation, recirculation, basal freeze-on and surface origin multiplicity. If these occur, the simple order theorem is inapplicable and the full material map must be used.
  \item \textbf{Inherited unsteady stratigraphy.} If the steady model preserves order, evaluate changes in ablation pattern, ice thickness, flow direction, divide position, bed geometry and basal traction. These mechanisms inject finite disturbances that can later be amplified by shear.
  \item \textbf{Active deformation.} If kinematic models underpredict observed folds, couple fabric and rheological heterogeneity to flow. The microstructural evidence makes this a likely contributor at least at centimetre scale.
\end{enumerate}

This hierarchy sharpens, rather than proves, the intuition that boundary-condition changes are required. They are effectively required in the regular shallow, non-stagnant regime because Equation~\eqref{eq:obstaclebound} leaves a large order margin. They are not mathematically required for every full-Stokes glacier: sufficiently cumulative extrusion or loss of unique throughflow can overturn steady isochrones.

\section{Numerical diagnostic for full-Stokes models}

The order criterion can be evaluated without explicitly solving the age equation or advecting a dense family of isochrones. Release trajectories from an accumulating inflow boundary and record \(x_0\), \(\dot a_0\) and \(u_{s0}\). Along each trajectory integrate
\begin{align}
\frac{\dd x}{\dd t}&=u,
&
\frac{\dd z}{\dd t}&=w,
\label{eq:trajectory}\\
\frac{\dd\Rpos}{\dd t}
&=\dot a_0u_{s0}\frac{u_z^+}{u^2},
&
\frac{\dd\Rneg}{\dd t}
&=\dot a_0u_{s0}\frac{u_z^-}{u^2},
\label{eq:PRode}
\end{align}
with \(\Rpos(0)=\Rneg(0)=0\). Then
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\Jord(t)=\frac{u(t)}{u_{s0}}
\left[1+\Rpos(t)-\Rneg(t)\right].
}
\label{eq:Jdiagnostic}
\end{equation}
The diagnostic is
\begin{equation}
\min_t\left[1+\Rpos(t)-\Rneg(t)\right]
\begin{cases}
>0, & \text{order preserved},\\
=0, & \text{first vertical tangent},\\
<0, & \text{overturned steady isochrone}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:numericaltest}
\end{equation}

A robust implementation should include four checks. First, compare Equation~\eqref{eq:Jdiagnostic} with \(-\dot a_0\tau_z\) from an independently solved steady age equation. Second, advect at least one pair of neighboring particles to verify the sign of \(\partial x/\partial x_0|_{\tau}\). Third, stop the downstream-coordinate integration when \(u\) approaches zero and diagnose recirculation with the full trajectory topology. Fourth, compute \(u_z\) using the finite-element gradient rather than differencing noisy interpolated velocities. Separate storage of \(\Rpos\) and \(\Rneg\) is preferable to integrating their difference because large compensating contributions can otherwise obscure numerical error.

For transient flow, Equation~\eqref{eq:Jdiagnostic} is not valid because the steady travel-time representation fails. The corresponding calculation should integrate the deformation of the two-parameter deposition map through time. Nevertheless, the steady order budget remains useful as a baseline and as a diagnostic applied to quasi-steady snapshots.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{A theorem about deposition order, not about curvature}

The curvature equation for an advected material line contains first and second spatial derivatives of velocity. It correctly shows that an obstacle can create curvature from an initially straight layer. The present order criterion answers a different question. A recumbent fold requires the horizontal projection of an equal-age surface to reverse, or equivalently \(\tau_z\) to change sign. An obstacle may generate large curvature while \(\Jord\) remains positive everywhere. This is the rigorous sense in which a steady bump can disturb isochrones without supplying the order-reversing seed required for a recumbent fold.

WBA's statement that steady-state layers are not passively folded can now be made conditional. If a unique steady age field exists and Equation~\eqref{eq:ordercondition} holds, exact steady stratigraphy is an invariant solution and remains ordered. A prescribed steady flow cannot spontaneously create a perturbation relative to that solution. However, arbitrary material lines placed in the same velocity field are not subject to this restriction, and the restriction can fail if cumulative reverse shear crosses the threshold or if the surface-origin coordinate ceases to be global.

\subsection{Why local extrusion is usually insufficient}

The full-Stokes literature demonstrates local extrusion but does not imply order reversal. Three effects protect the isochrone. First, deposition provides a finite unit margin in Equation~\eqref{eq:JPR}. Second, ordinary surface-fast shear adds \(\Rpos\). Third, reverse shear around a localized or periodic obstacle is short-lived and can cancel between stoss and lee sides. The bound in Equation~\eqref{eq:obstaclebound} quantifies the first two effects, while Equation~\eqref{eq:periodiccancel} quantifies the third in a perturbative setting.

Near stagnation the protection weakens sharply. The \(u^{-3}\) spatial weighting and \(u^{-2}\) temporal weighting imply that a slowly moving basal zone can accumulate a large order change even when the pointwise shear is moderate. This is also the regime in which age becomes large, numerical conditioning deteriorates and recirculation becomes possible. A convincing steady-obstacle explanation for Allan Hills folding should therefore identify a specific slow reverse-shear pathway and demonstrate that it crosses Equation~\eqref{eq:verticalcondition}; showing \(u_z<0\) is insufficient.

\subsection{Boundary changes as fold seeds}

Time-dependent boundary processes remain the most natural route to finite inherited disturbances. Surface sublimation can truncate and steepen layers; migration of the ablation boundary can leave that geometry to be buried or sheared. Basal melt and freeze-on alter source and sink topology. Divide migration changes the reference velocity profile. Traveling traction anomalies create a moving-frame critical layer and material trapping. Bedrock uplift or rotation changes both geometry and stress. Each mechanism breaks at least one assumption behind the steady order theorem and can therefore create a nonzero perturbation relative to the current steady stratigraphy.

The order framework does not identify which mechanism operated at Allan Hills. It supplies a rejection test. If a high-resolution, history-consistent full-Stokes model of fixed geometry satisfies \(\Jord>0\) by a wide margin on all relevant trajectories, then steady topographic draping is excluded as the cause of recumbent age reversals. The remaining explanation must invoke time dependence, multiple material sources, active rheology, three-dimensional exchange or structural discontinuity.

\subsection{Novelty and relationship to established theory}

Several components of this manuscript are established. Equation~\eqref{eq:agepsiJacobian} is a standard consequence of steady incompressible transport. Logarithmic flux coordinates and characteristic age solutions were developed by \textcite{ParreninHindmarshRemy2006} and \textcite{ParreninHindmarsh2007}, and the latter paper contains both the monotonic-shear corollary and the path dependence of isochrone slope. The vertical thinning function was derived in the same framework by \textcite{Parrenin2013} and is now computed operationally by \textcite{ParreninChungMartin2025}; Equation~\eqref{eq:thinningrelation} shows that the present order factor is its inverse in the ordered regime. The need for a transient wrinkle and the competition between shear and extension are due to \textcite{Waddington2001}. Finite-strain amplification is treated by \textcite{JacobsonWaddington2004}. Local extrusion and rough-bed recirculation are known full-Stokes phenomena \parencite{Gudmundsson1997linear,Gudmundsson1997nonlinear}.

The specific analytical contribution is therefore not the age--streamfunction coordinate system, the thinning factor or the monotonic-shear theorem. It is the explicit physical-space identity that writes the signed inverse thinning as a cumulative \(u_z/u^3\) integral, its positive--negative decomposition in Equations~\eqref{eq:Pdef}--\eqref{eq:ordercondition}, and the mechanical consequences drawn from that decomposition. A targeted search of the steady-isocrone, age-model and extrusion-flow literature did not identify the same reverse-shear budget, the full-Stokes residual threshold in Equation~\eqref{eq:FSfailure}, the localized-obstacle bound in Equation~\eqref{eq:obstaclebound}, the plug-flow cancellation result in Equation~\eqref{eq:periodiccancel}, or the split trajectory diagnostic in Equation~\eqref{eq:Jdiagnostic}. Priority for such elementary identities is difficult to establish exhaustively, so these claims should be read as a cautious reformulation and extension of Parrenin--Hindmarsh theory, not as a new universal theorem about incompressible Stokes flow.

The strongest future novelty would come from applying the diagnostic to realistic Allan Hills geometry and history. A model that either proves a large positive order margin or demonstrates a trajectory crossing \(\Jord=0\) would resolve whether fixed roughness is dynamically adequate. Coupling that calculation to observed layer attitudes, age reversals and fabric would connect a mathematical sign criterion to a specific paleoclimate archive.

\subsection{Limitations}

The analysis neglects diffusion of age markers, fracture and discontinuous displacement. It assumes a smooth two-dimensional velocity field and a single-valued accumulating inflow branch. The full-Stokes decomposition assumes isotropic scalar viscosity. It does not establish a universal mechanical bound on \(R_{\mathrm{FS}}\), because none follows from the Stokes equations alone. The localized-obstacle estimate depends on externally supplied bounds on minimum speed, deformation scale and reverse shear. The periodic cancellation result is first order and can be destroyed by asymmetry or nonlinear geometry.

Most importantly, the theorem diagnoses overturning of a continuous material isochrone. An observed age reversal can also result from faulting, unconformity, multiple source regions, freeze-on ice, sampling across a sharply dipping but discontinuous structure, or unresolved three-dimensional geometry. The order diagnostic should therefore be used with structural observations rather than as a stand-alone interpretation.

\section{Conclusions}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item A steady two-dimensional isochrone preserves depositional order if and only if its signed order factor
  \[
  \Jord=\frac{u}{u_{s0}}
  \left[1+\dot a_0u_{s0}\int_{x_0}^{x}\frac{u_z}{u^3}\,\dd\xi\right]
  \]
  remains positive. The equivalent observable condition is \(\tau_z<0\), and in the ordered regime \(\Jord\) is the reciprocal of the established vertical thinning function.

  \item Pointwise surface-fast shear, \(u_z\geq0\), is sufficient but not necessary. The exact condition is cumulative:
  \[
  \Rneg<1+\Rpos.
  \]
  Local extrusion is harmless unless its weighted path integral erases the unit depositional margin and all accumulated normal shear.

  \item Full-Stokes mechanics does not guarantee the condition for arbitrary glacier geometry. Gravity-driven shear favors order, but longitudinal stress, nonhydrostatic pressure and horizontal gradients of vertical velocity can generate reverse shear. Stagnation and recirculation invalidate the one-way coordinate construction entirely.

  \item The shallow-ice regime preserves order. A non-stagnant localized obstacle obeys the sufficient bound
  \[
  \Rneg\leq C\frac{r_s}{r^3}\frac{\dot a_0}{U}\frac{\ell_-}{d},
  \]
  and small-amplitude periodic roughness about a uniform plug-flow background has no first-order net order change over a complete wavelength.

  \item The theory converts the intuition that fixed obstacles produce draping rather than folds into a conditional, testable statement. In ordinary fast, shallow throughflow, boundary or rheological changes are effectively required to seed recumbent folding. In slow, rough, near-stagnant full-Stokes flow, fixed geometry remains a viable mechanism until the cumulative diagnostic is evaluated.

  \item For COLDEX, the decisive next calculation is to integrate \(\Rpos\), \(\Rneg\) and \(\Jord\) along reconstructed source-to-core trajectories at Allan Hills. This will distinguish steady topographic deformation from folding that requires ablation history, basal mass exchange, traction change, geometry change, anisotropy or three-dimensional flow.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Inverse-coordinate identities}
\label{app:inverse}

Equation~\eqref{eq:agepsiJacobian} can be written
\begin{equation}
\begin{pmatrix}
\tau_x & \tau_z\\
\psi_x & \psi_z
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
\dd x\\
\dd z
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\dd\tau\\
\dd\psi
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\det=1.
\end{equation}
The inverse transformation is
\begin{equation}
\begin{pmatrix}
\dd x\\
\dd z
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\psi_z & -\tau_z\\
-\psi_x & \tau_x
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
\dd\tau\\
\dd\psi
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
 u & -\tau_z\\
 w & \tau_x
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
\dd\tau\\
\dd\psi
\end{pmatrix}.
\label{eq:inversemap}
\end{equation}
Therefore
\begin{equation}
 x_{\tau}|_{\psi}=u,
 \quad
 z_{\tau}|_{\psi}=w,
 \quad
 x_{\psi}|_{\tau}=-\tau_z,
 \quad
 z_{\psi}|_{\tau}=\tau_x.
\end{equation}
The first two identities state that advancing one unit in age at fixed streamline follows the velocity for one unit of time. The latter two specify the equal-age tangent. Multiplication by \(\dd\psi/\dd x_0=\dot a_0\) yields Equation~\eqref{eq:Jage} and the corresponding vertical tangent component.

\section{Extension to steady variable-density flow}
\label{app:density}

Below the firn, glacier ice is accurately treated as incompressible with nearly constant density. The coordinate construction extends to a steady variable-density flow satisfying
\begin{equation}
\nabla\cdot(\rho\uvec)=0.
\end{equation}
Define the mass-flux streamfunction by
\begin{equation}
\rho u=\psi_z,
\qquad
\rho w=-\psi_x.
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial(\tau,\psi)}{\partial(x,z)}
=\rho(u\tau_x+w\tau_z)=\rho.
\end{equation}
The mapping remains nonsingular for \(\rho>0\), and the same order interpretation follows after replacing volume flux by mass flux. The constant-density formula used in the main text is therefore not conceptually tied to strict incompressibility, although the weights in the cumulative integral acquire density factors.

\section{First-order periodic cancellation about plug flow}
\label{app:periodic}

Let
\begin{equation}
 u(x,z;\epsilon_b)=U_b+\epsilon_b u_1(x,z)+O(\epsilon_b^2),
 \qquad U_b>0,
\end{equation}
where \(U_b\) is spatially uniform and \(u_1\) is periodic in \(x\) with wavelength \(2\pi/k\) and zero horizontal mean at each elevation. Then
\begin{equation}
 u_z=\epsilon_b u_{1,z}+O(\epsilon_b^2),
 \qquad
 u^{-3}=U_b^{-3}+O(\epsilon_b).
\end{equation}
Because the background integrand is identically zero, the \(O(\epsilon_b)\) displacement of the trajectory affects the integral only at \(O(\epsilon_b^2)\). Evaluating the first-order term along the unperturbed streamline \(z=z_0\) gives
\begin{equation}
\int_x^{x+2\pi/k}\frac{u_z}{u^3}\,\dd\xi
=\frac{\epsilon_b}{U_b^3}
\int_x^{x+2\pi/k}u_{1,z}(\xi,z_0)\,\dd\xi
+O(\epsilon_b^2)
=O(\epsilon_b^2),
\end{equation}
because differentiation with respect to \(z\) preserves the zero horizontal mean of \(u_1\). This establishes Equation~\eqref{eq:periodiccancel}. For a vertically sheared background, trajectory displacement can act on the nonzero background integrand at first order, so the cancellation does not follow without additional assumptions.
